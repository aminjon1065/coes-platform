# CoESCD Platform — Full System Integration, Deployment, and Roadmap

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This document is the capstone of the CoESCD platform architecture series. It integrates the six domain-level architecture documents — Architecture Foundation, RBAC and Organizational Model, EDMS and Workflow Architecture, Task Management Architecture, Chat and Real-time Architecture, and GIS, Analytics, and AI Forecasting Architecture — into a single coherent system design, deployment strategy, and phased implementation roadmap.

The platform is a unified digital ecosystem for the Committee of Emergency Situations and Civil Defense under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan. It is a government-grade, mission-critical, internally deployed enterprise system. It must serve as the operational backbone of an emergency management institution — reliable under crisis conditions, secure against internal and external threats, auditable to the standard of government accountability, and maintainable by a realistic in-house engineering team over a multi-year lifespan.

The architecture recommendation is a **phased modular monolith with three independently deployed runtime components from day one**, evolving toward a domain-partitioned service topology as organizational and engineering maturity grows. The three extracted components that are architecturally independent from day one are: the Real-time Gateway (WebSocket connection management), the GIS and Analytics Service (spatial data infrastructure with its own PostGIS database), and the Media Subsystem (SFU-based audio/video infrastructure). All transactional business domains — IAM, Organization, Authorization, EDMS, Task Management, File Management, Notification, Audit, and Administration — are deployed as a modular monolith in Phase 1 through Phase 3, with domain extraction occurring only when specific operational evidence justifies it.

The phased roadmap spans six phases over approximately 24-30 months, starting with a governance and infrastructure foundation and delivering production-capable capabilities in a sequence that prioritizes: first, the organizational and access foundation; second, the formal documentary and task execution workflows; third, real-time communication; fourth, spatial and analytical intelligence; and fifth, AI-assisted forecasting. Each phase delivers standalone operational value. No phase creates architectural debt that blocks a subsequent phase.

## 2. Full Platform Mission and Integration Goals

### 2.1 What Integration Means for This Platform

Integration in the CoESCD context is not a technical exercise in connecting APIs. It is the enforcement of organizational coherence across digital workflows. A document approval in the EDMS must automatically generate an execution task in Task Management. That task must carry the classification level of the originating document. The task's geographic scope must be visible on the GIS operational map. The executor's progress must be notifiable through the communication system. The entire lifecycle must be auditable from document registration to task completion. Without integration, these are five separate systems that a user must navigate manually. With integration, they form one institutional workflow.

The integration goals are therefore organizational, not just technical:

**Goal 1 — Workflow continuity across domains.** A process that begins in one domain must continue naturally in another without the user losing context or the system losing accountability. Document resolution to task creation to communication to completion is one workflow, not four.

**Goal 2 — Single organizational model.** The organizational hierarchy defined in the RBAC domain is the one source of truth for all access decisions, all routing rules, all escalation chains, and all reporting relationships across the entire platform. No domain maintains a shadow copy of the org structure.

**Goal 3 — Unified audit trail.** An auditor investigating a specific incident — a document, a task, a communication, a spatial decision — can traverse the full chain of events across domains in the audit system without switching tools.

**Goal 4 — Consistent security posture.** A user's clearance level, role assignments, and delegation status have the same meaning and the same enforcement consequence in every domain. A classified document is classified consistently in EDMS, in File Management, in GIS risk layers derived from it, and in the communication channel linked to its workflow.

**Goal 5 — Operational resilience.** The platform must serve its most critical function — supporting emergency response coordination — precisely when conditions are most adverse. Integration must be designed for graceful degradation: if the GIS service is unavailable, EDMS and Task Management continue to function. If the media subsystem is under load, chat continues unaffected.

## 3. Recommended Full-System Architecture Style

### 3.1 Final Recommendation

**A modular monolith core with three independently deployed runtime services**, evolving through evidence-based domain extraction toward a distributed domain topology.

This is the correct architecture for CoESCD at this stage of platform maturity for the following reasons, stated as falsifiable propositions:

**Proposition 1:** The CoESCD development team is not ten independent product teams. It is one or two teams building a first-generation internal platform. Microservices at this scale front-load operational complexity — distributed tracing, service mesh, inter-service TLS, distributed transaction management — with no feature benefit. The modular monolith provides equivalent domain isolation with dramatically lower operational overhead.

**Proposition 2:** The domain boundaries established in the architecture documents are intellectually sound but unproven in production. The first year of operation will reveal where the boundaries are wrong — where two domains are too tightly coupled to remain separate, or where one domain needs to be split. Building the boundaries as internal module contracts within a monolith allows correction. Building them as network-separated microservices makes correction expensive.

**Proposition 3:** The three extracted components (Real-time Gateway, GIS/Analytics Service, Media Subsystem) are extracted not because microservices are better, but because their runtime characteristics make sharing a process with the business logic monolith technically incorrect. The WebSocket gateway maintains long-lived connections at a scale that requires different process management. The GIS service requires PostGIS, specialized memory tuning, and a spatial query processing profile incompatible with OLTP tuning. The media SFU is CPU-bound in a way that must be isolated from the I/O-bound business logic.

**Proposition 4:** The modular monolith is not a permanent state. When a specific domain demonstrates — through production evidence — that it needs an independent deployment cadence, independent scaling, or independent technology choices, it is extracted. The discipline of enforcing module boundaries from day one makes this extraction a refactoring exercise, not a rewrite.

### 3.2 What Stays Together (Monolith Core)

The following domains are deployed as a single application process in Phase 1-3, with internal module boundaries strictly enforced:

- Identity and Access Management (IAM)
- Organization and Department Management
- User Management
- Authorization
- EDMS and Document Workflow
- Task Management
- File Management
- Notification
- Audit Event Emission
- Administration and Platform Settings

### 3.3 What Is Extracted from Day One

- **Real-time Gateway:** WebSocket connection management, presence state, event fan-out. Extracted because runtime characteristics (thousands of persistent connections, Redis-backed horizontal scaling, distinct operational profile) are incompatible with the monolith.
- **GIS and Analytics Service:** PostGIS, spatial queries, tile serving, analytical store (TimescaleDB), ingestion pipelines. Extracted because of specialized infrastructure requirements, spatial query load isolation, and distinct deployment topology.
- **Media Subsystem:** Mediasoup SFU, TURN/STUN servers, recording pipeline. Extracted because media processing is CPU-bound and bandwidth-intensive — it must be isolated from the business logic to prevent resource contention.

### 3.4 What May Be Extracted in Phase 4+ (Evidence-Based)

- **Chat/Messaging Service:** If message throughput grows to a level that creates database contention with the other domains, the Communication domain's PostgreSQL schema can be migrated to a dedicated instance. The service extraction follows after database separation.
- **AI/ML Forecasting Service:** Training pipelines and model serving are already logically separated in the AI/ML architecture. Physical extraction to a dedicated service becomes necessary when ML training requires dedicated GPU or high-memory compute nodes.
- **Audit Domain:** If the audit log write volume (driven by platform growth) creates I/O pressure on the shared database, the Audit domain's schema is the first candidate for migration to a dedicated append-only database instance.

### 4.3 Integration Patterns Summary

**Monolith internal integration:** Direct in-process function calls with HTTP-shaped contracts. No serialization overhead. Module A calls Module B's service layer directly. The contract is the same as if Module B were a separate service — arguments and return types are defined, not shared object graphs.

**Monolith to Real-time Gateway:** The monolith publishes events to RabbitMQ. The Real-time Gateway subscribes to relevant exchange topics and delivers events to connected clients. This is the primary fan-out mechanism for chat messages, notifications, and presence updates.

**Monolith to GIS Service:** HTTP REST calls for spatial queries (incident location enrichment, proximity alerts). GIS Service to Monolith: HTTP REST calls for authorization decisions and user/org data. Event bus for analytical data: the Analytics ingestion pipeline subscribes to `incident.reported`, `task.completed`, `document.registered` events from the monolith.

**Monolith to Media Subsystem:** HTTP REST for call session management (create session, end session, retrieve recording reference). WebSocket signaling for SDP/ICE exchange (routed through the Real-time Gateway signaling path to the SFU).

## 5. Shared Platform Services

Shared platform services are capabilities used by multiple domains that must be centralized to ensure consistency, avoid duplication, and maintain a single governance point.

### 5.1 Authentication Service (IAM Domain)

**Why centralized:** Every API request from every client must be authenticated against the same identity store with the same token validation rules. Distributed authentication — each domain validating tokens independently — creates inconsistency risk: a session revoked in IAM must be immediately revoked everywhere. Centralized token validation (at the API Gateway) with a shared IAM validation endpoint achieves this.

**Used by:** All domains via the API Gateway (token validation on every request) and directly for token refresh operations.

### 5.2 Authorization Service (Authorization Domain)

**Why centralized:** The three-layer authorization model (RBAC + hierarchical authority + classification) is complex to implement correctly. Any domain that implements its own subset of this model will inevitably drift from the authoritative model, creating security gaps. The single `can(userId, action, resourceType, resourceId)` API is the enforcement point for the entire platform.

**Used by:** Every domain before executing any sensitive operation. Result is cached per user session with short TTL.

### 5.3 Organization Directory (OrgManagement Domain)

**Why centralized:** The organizational hierarchy — departments, positions, reporting lines, command chains — is the structural foundation for routing, authorization scope, workflow chains, and escalation logic. Every domain that makes routing or authority decisions reads this single source of truth.

**Used by:** EDMS (approval chain resolution), TaskManagement (assignment authority validation), Authorization (hierarchical scope computation), Notification (routing notifications to positions), Real-time Gateway (department channel membership derivation).

### 5.4 Notification Service (Notification Domain)

**Why centralized:** Notification delivery involves channel selection (in-app, email, SMS), user preferences, batching/throttling, and delivery confirmation. If each domain implements its own notification logic, users receive duplicated notifications, preferences are not respected consistently, and there is no unified visibility into notification health.

**Used by:** EDMS, TaskManagement, Chat Domain, GIS Service, Administration — all publish notification events to a shared exchange. The Notification Domain handles delivery.

### 5.5 Audit Service (Audit Domain)

**Why centralized:** Security audit records must be immutable and must not be under the control of the domains whose actions they record. A centralized, append-only audit store with its own database access controls ensures that no domain can modify or suppress its own audit trail. The audit event schema is defined centrally so all domains produce structurally consistent records.

**Used by:** All domains emit audit events via the platform event bus. The Audit Domain is a consumer only — it does not respond to requests from other domains.

### 5.6 File Attachment Service (FileManagement Domain)

**Why centralized:** File storage, virus scanning, versioning, access control, and pre-signed URL generation for secure download are capabilities needed by EDMS (document attachments), TaskManagement (task attachments), Chat (file shares), and GIS (dataset files). Centralizing file handling ensures consistent virus scanning, consistent classification enforcement on files, and unified storage management.

**Used by:** EDMS, TaskManagement, Chat Domain, GIS Service, Conferencing (recordings).

### 5.7 Event Bus (RabbitMQ / Kafka)

**Why centralized:** Asynchronous integration between domains requires a reliable message delivery infrastructure. A shared event bus with defined exchange topology ensures at-least-once delivery, enables new consumers to subscribe without modifying producers, and provides a single operational surface for monitoring message health.

**Used by:** All domains as producers (business events) and consumers (reacting to other domains' events).

### 5.8 Search Service (OpenSearch)

**Why centralized:** Full-text search across documents, tasks, messages, and GIS metadata requires a unified index with authorization-aware filtering. A shared search cluster with per-domain indexes (documents index, tasks index, messages index, GIS catalog index) provides cross-domain search capability while maintaining access control context.

**Used by:** EDMS, TaskManagement, Chat Domain, GIS Service, Administration.

### 5.9 Configuration Service

**Why centralized:** Environment-specific configuration (database URLs, service endpoints, feature flags, integration keys) must be managed consistently across all components. Distributed configuration — each service managing its own — creates operational risk: a misconfigured service in one environment introduces inconsistency that is difficult to diagnose.

**Pattern:** Environment variables injected at runtime for Phase 1 (12-factor app pattern). Secrets vault (HashiCorp Vault) for Phase 2 for secrets specifically. Feature flags via a lightweight configuration store.

## 6. Cross-Module Integration Model

### 6.1 Integration Architecture Principles

**Principle 1 — The event bus is for notifications, not for commands.** Events inform other domains that something happened. They do not command other domains to do things. "document.approved" is an event. "create_task" is a command and must be a synchronous call, not an event, so the caller knows whether the task was created.

**Principle 2 — Synchronous calls for transactional consistency, events for eventual propagation.** If Domain A's action fails when Domain B is unavailable, use a synchronous call. If Domain A's action can succeed independently and Domain B needs to be informed afterward, use an event.

**Principle 3 — Each domain owns its data; reads happen through service APIs.** Domain B reads Domain A's data by calling Domain A's API, not by querying Domain A's database schema. This is the boundary that makes domains extractable.

**Principle 4 — Correlation IDs flow across the full chain.** Every operation — from the initial HTTP request to every event it produces to every downstream action those events trigger — carries the same correlation ID. The full chain is reconstructible in the observability system.

### 6.2 Key Cross-Module Flows

---

**Flow 1: Document Resolution → Task Creation → Notification → GIS Visualization**

1. Chairman issues a Resolution on an incoming emergency notification in EDMS
2. EDMS creates an Executor Assignment record (EDMS domain) synchronously
3. EDMS publishes `resolution.assignment_created` event to the event bus with: document ID, resolution ID, assignment ID, assigned position, deadline, geographic scope (if the resolution references an affected area)
4. TaskManagement subscribes to this event and creates a Task record (TaskManagement domain) linked to the EDMS assignment
5. TaskManagement publishes `task.created` event with: task ID, assigned position, deadline, priority, geographic reference
6. Notification Domain subscribes to `task.created` and dispatches assignment notifications to the assigned position's current occupant via the Real-time Gateway (if online) and/or email
7. GIS Service subscribes to `task.created` and adds the task's geographic reference to the "active response tasks" operational layer (task location appears on the map within seconds)
8. Real-time Gateway delivers the notification event to the assigned user's WebSocket connection

**Correlation:** A single correlation ID links the Resolution event, the task creation, the notification, and the GIS layer update — all reconstructible as one chain in the audit log.

---

**Flow 2: GIS Incident Report → Analytics → Emergency Alert → Task Creation**

1. Field analyst submits an incident report via the GIS/Analytics data collection form
2. Analytics Domain validates, enriches (administrative region from spatial join), and persists the incident record
3. Analytics Domain publishes `incident.reported` event with: incident ID, location, type, severity, affected positions derived from spatial containment
4. GIS Service updates the operational incident layer (incident marker appears on the map within 30 seconds)
5. Notification Domain receives `incident.reported` with affected positions and delivers emergency notifications to those positions via Real-time Gateway and Emergency Broadcast Channel
6. The responsible department head receives the notification, opens the incident on the GIS map, reviews the spatial context, and creates a Response Task in TaskManagement manually (or the incident type triggers an automatic task template if configured)
7. Audit Domain receives: incident creation event, notification dispatch event, task creation event — all linked by correlation ID

---

**Flow 3: Document Attachment → File Storage → Access Control → Audit Trail**

1. Analyst attaches a classified file to an EDMS document during drafting
2. Client requests a pre-signed upload URL from FileManagement via the EDMS interface
3. FileManagement generates a pre-signed URL scoped to the user's identity, records the pending upload
4. Client uploads directly to MinIO using the pre-signed URL
5. MinIO notifies FileManagement of successful storage; FileManagement triggers virus scanning
6. FileManagement stores file metadata with: file ID, checksum, uploader, classification (derived from the parent document's classification)
7. EDMS receives the file reference from FileManagement and stores it as an attachment record
8. FileManagement sets the file's access permissions to match the document's access rules (via Authorization domain)
9. Audit Domain receives: `file.uploaded` event (FileManagement), `document.attachment_added` event (EDMS) — both linked to the same document ID and correlation ID

---

**Flow 4: Conference Recording → File Storage → Access Control → Audit Trail**

1. Conference organizer initiates recording in the Conferencing interface
2. Recording initiation is logged: `conference.recording_started` event to Audit Domain; all participants receive a system notification ("This conference is being recorded")
3. Recording Service (Media Subsystem) captures streams from the SFU
4. Conference ends; Recording Service finalizes the recording file
5. Recording Service calls FileManagement's upload API with the recording file, classification (inherited from the conference session's classification), and participant list as initial access recipients
6. FileManagement stores the recording in the recordings MinIO bucket with server-side encryption
7. Communication Domain receives `conference.recording_ready` event and updates the call session record with the recording reference
8. Participants with recording access can retrieve the pre-signed playback URL from FileManagement via the Communication domain interface
9. Audit Domain receives: `conference.recording_stored` event, `file.access_permission_set` event

---

**Flow 5: Task Discussion → Chat Channel → File Attachment → Notification**

1. Task is created; TaskManagement publishes `task.created` event
2. Communication Domain receives the event and creates a Task-Linked Channel with the assigning authority and executor as initial members
3. Executor opens the task and navigates to the linked discussion channel
4. Executor sends a message with a file attachment (a preliminary field report)
5. Client uploads the file to FileManagement via pre-signed URL; file reference is attached to the message
6. Chat Domain stores the message record with the file reference
7. Chat Domain publishes `message.created` event to the event bus
8. Real-time Gateway delivers the message to all online channel members via WebSocket
9. Notification Domain delivers offline-user notifications to the assigning authority (who is not currently online)
10. FileManagement sets the file's access permissions to match the channel's access context (same users who can read the channel can read the attached file)
11. Audit Domain receives: `message.sent` (with file reference), `file.access_permission_set`

---

**Flow 6: AI Flood Risk Prediction → Analyst Review → Operational Layer Publication → Alert**

1. Scheduled ML prediction pipeline runs; flood risk scores are computed for all watersheds
2. AI/ML service writes prediction outputs to the prediction store and publishes `prediction.batch_complete` event
3. GIS Domain receives the event and creates draft risk layer records (status: DRAFT, not visible to operational users)
4. GIS Domain publishes `risk_layer.draft_ready` notification to the analyst review queue
5. Designated analyst opens the draft layer in the GIS interface, reviews predictions against current conditions
6. Analyst approves the layer; GIS Domain transitions the layer to OPERATIONAL status
7. GIS Domain publishes `risk_layer.published` event; tile cache for the risk layer is invalidated; new tiles are generated from updated risk scores
8. Notification Domain receives the event and delivers alerts to positions responsible for high-risk regions
9. Communication Domain delivers alerts through the relevant Emergency Broadcast Channels
10. Audit Domain receives: `prediction.reviewed` (analyst ID, layer ID, approval action, timestamp)

### 6.3 Coupling Analysis

**Tightly coupled (synchronous calls required):**
- Any operation requiring authorization validation (synchronous `can()` call)
- EDMS → TaskManagement for task creation from resolution (must succeed or fail atomically)
- Any file operation → FileManagement (file ID returned must be stored in the same transaction)

**Loosely coupled (asynchronous events sufficient):**
- Any domain → Notification (delivery is best-effort and asynchronous by nature)
- Any domain → Audit (eventual consistency is acceptable; audit events are never dropped due to durable queuing)
- EDMS → GIS (document spatial references are enrichment, not transactionally critical)
- TaskManagement → Analytics (performance metrics are derived from events, not from synchronous queries)

### 7.2 Data Ownership and Cross-Domain Access

The fundamental rule: a domain queries data it does not own by calling the owning domain's service API. Database-level cross-schema queries are prohibited by PostgreSQL role grants — each domain's application database role has SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE only on its own schema. Cross-schema JOINs at the database level are architecturally forbidden.

The practical consequence: when the EDMS needs to display the name of the position a document is routed to, it does not join the `org` schema — it calls OrgManagement's `getPositionById()` API. The result may be cached for the session. This is the discipline that makes domain extraction feasible.

### 7.3 Operational vs. Analytical Data

**Operational data** lives in the transactional PostgreSQL database. It is normalized, ACID-transactional, and optimized for individual record operations. All current-state records (active documents, active tasks, current user sessions, active organizational structure) live here.

**Analytical data** is derived from operational data through ETL pipelines and lives in TimescaleDB. It is denormalized, pre-aggregated, and optimized for time-filtered aggregation queries. Historical incident records, performance metrics, and trend data live here. Dashboard queries hit TimescaleDB, not the operational database.

**The critical discipline:** Reports and dashboards never query the operational database directly for aggregation. All analytical queries go to the analytical store. The operational database is sized and tuned for transactional throughput; the analytical store is sized and tuned for analytical throughput. They are scaled independently.

### 7.4 Backup and Archival Strategy

**Continuous WAL archiving** for PostgreSQL (both primary and GIS databases) to a dedicated backup storage volume. WAL archives provide point-in-time recovery capability — the database can be restored to any second within the retention window (minimum 7 days of WAL archives).

**Daily full backup snapshots** of all databases at off-peak hours, stored in the `/backups` MinIO bucket with server-side encryption. Full snapshots are retained for 90 days.

**Monthly archival snapshots** retained for 7 years (aligned with government record-keeping obligations). Monthly snapshots are compressed and stored in a separate long-term archive volume.

**MinIO object replication** to a secondary storage node for all buckets containing government records (documents, recordings, exports). Replication is synchronous for the `/documents` and `/recordings` buckets (data loss is unacceptable); asynchronous for `/gis-datasets` and `/exports` (loss is recoverable through reprocessing).

**Recovery Time Objective (RTO):** The operational database: < 30 minutes from the point of a hardware failure (automatic failover to hot standby via Patroni). The full platform from a backup: < 4 hours for critical services (EDMS, TaskManagement), < 8 hours for all services.

**Recovery Point Objective (RPO):** Transactional data: < 30 seconds (WAL shipping latency to standby). Non-critical analytical data: < 15 minutes (batch pipeline interval).

## 8. Deployment Architecture

### 8.1 Physical Topology

The platform is deployed on-premises in CoESCD's government data center. The deployment is structured in three logical tiers:

**Tier 1 — Edge and Gateway**
- 2× Nginx instances (active-passive with keepalived VIP) for TLS termination and load balancing
- API Gateway functionality embedded in the Nginx configuration and the monolith's gateway module
- External firewall boundary

**Tier 2 — Application Servers**
- 2-3× Application server nodes (primary monolith instances, load-balanced by Nginx)
- 2× Real-time Gateway instances (WebSocket, behind load balancer with sticky sessions for connection duration)
- 1-2× GIS/Analytics Service instances (behind load balancer for API, tile serving)
- 1× AI/ML Service instance (standalone initially, extraction to dedicated hardware in Phase 5)
- 2× Mediasoup SFU instances (media cluster, session-distributed)
- 1× TURN/STUN Server (network edge, publicly accessible within government network)
- 1× Recording Service instance

**Tier 3 — Data Services**
- 1× PostgreSQL Primary + 1× Hot Standby (Patroni-managed) + 1× Read Replica
- 1× PostGIS Primary + 1× Read Replica
- 1× TimescaleDB instance
- 3× Redis Cluster nodes (minimum for cluster HA)
- 3× RabbitMQ cluster nodes (mirrored queues)
- 4× MinIO nodes (distributed mode with erasure coding)
- 3× OpenSearch nodes (minimum for production cluster)
- 1× Airflow instance (pipeline orchestration)

**Observability Stack (dedicated nodes or co-located with monitoring server)**
- 1× Prometheus + Alertmanager
- 1× Grafana
- 1× Loki + Promtail agents on all nodes
- 1× Jaeger (distributed tracing collector)

### 8.2 Container Strategy

All application components are containerized using Docker. Container orchestration uses **Docker Compose for Phase 1** (operationally simple, familiar to most DevOps engineers, adequate for the initial deployment scale) transitioning to **Kubernetes for Phase 3+** when the number of independently managed services justifies the orchestration overhead.

The containerization provides:
- Reproducible deployments across environments
- Clean dependency isolation
- Straightforward horizontal scaling (add containers)
- Simplified rollback (revert to previous container image)

The deliberate choice of Docker Compose over Kubernetes in Phase 1 reflects the operational reality: Kubernetes is a powerful platform that requires dedicated expertise to operate safely. Deploying 15 services on Kubernetes without a platform engineering team creates operational risk that exceeds the benefit. Docker Compose on well-provisioned bare metal or VMs is a production-ready deployment pattern at CoESCD's scale.

### 8.3 API Gateway and Routing

Nginx handles all client-to-server routing with the following routing rules:

- `/api/*` → Load balanced to monolith application instances (HTTP/1.1)
- `/ws/*` → Load balanced with sticky sessions to Real-time Gateway instances (WebSocket upgrade)
- `/gis/*` → Load balanced to GIS Service instances (tile serving, spatial API)
- `/media/*` → WebRTC signaling and session management (routed through Real-time Gateway)
- `/files/*` → FileManagement API on monolith, with MinIO pre-signed URL generation

TLS termination at Nginx. All backend connections within the server tier use unencrypted HTTP on a trusted private network. Backend TLS would add CPU overhead with no security benefit on a controlled internal network.

## 9. Environment Strategy

### 9.1 Environment Tiers

**Local Development (developer workstation):**
Docker Compose configuration with: PostgreSQL + PostGIS, Redis single node, RabbitMQ single node, MinIO single node, stub services for external dependencies (mock GIS tile server, mock AI prediction service). Hot reload enabled. No media server required — development uses mock call sessions. Full observability stack is optional for local development.

**Test / CI Environment (automated):**
Ephemeral environments spun up for each CI pipeline run. Uses the same Docker Compose configuration as local development with a sanitized data set. All integration tests, service boundary tests, and security unit tests run here. Environment is destroyed after each pipeline run. No persistent data.

**Staging Environment (persistent, near-production):**
A production-mirror environment running on hardware comparable to production. All production services deployed. Anonymized production data subset loaded. Used for: pre-release integration testing, performance profiling, new feature validation by internal users, training new staff, and disaster recovery drill target. Configuration is managed identically to production (same configuration management tooling, same secrets vault). No shortcuts on staging — the purpose is to catch what production testing misses.

**Production Environment:**
The full deployment topology described in Section 8.1. Configuration is managed through the secrets vault and environment-specific configuration files maintained in a private configuration repository (separate from the application source repository). Production configuration changes require approval before application.

**GIS/ML Isolated Environment (Phase 4+):**
For ML training runs that require significant compute resources, a dedicated ML training environment is provisioned separately from the production GIS service. Training runs on historical data snapshots that are copied from production. Training environment has no direct network access to production systems — model artifacts are transferred through a controlled model registry after training completes.

### 9.2 Configuration Management

Configuration is managed at three levels:

**Level 1 — Application defaults:** Hardcoded sane defaults in the application code. These are values that are safe in any environment (timeout values, retry counts, pagination defaults).

**Level 2 — Environment-specific configuration:** Environment variables injected at container startup. Non-secret configuration (feature flags, service URLs, log levels, cache TTLs). Stored in a configuration repository and applied through deployment scripts.

**Level 3 — Secrets:** Database credentials, JWT signing keys, MinIO access keys, external API tokens. Stored in HashiCorp Vault (Phase 2; environment variables for Phase 1). Injected into the container environment at startup through the Vault agent sidecar (Phase 2+) or deployment scripts (Phase 1).

The discipline: no secret appears in source code, in Docker images, or in git history. A repository scan for hardcoded credentials runs as a CI gate.

## 10. Security Architecture

### 10.1 Trust Boundaries and Enforcement Points

```
INTERNET / GOVERNMENT INTRANET
        │
        ▼
[EXTERNAL FIREWALL] ← First trust boundary
        │ Only 443 (HTTPS/WSS) and specific government network ports
        ▼
[Nginx Edge Layer] ← TLS termination
        │ All traffic validated for TLS certificate
        ▼
[API Gateway Module] ← Second trust boundary: JWT validation
        │ Every request carries valid JWT or is rejected
        ▼
[Application Services] ← Third enforcement: Authorization domain
        │ Every sensitive operation checks can()
        ▼
[Database Layer] ← Fourth enforcement: Schema-level RBAC
        │ Each service connects as a role with schema-scoped grants
        ▼
[Audit Domain] ← Sink: all significant events recorded
```

**External firewall:** Drops all traffic except HTTPS (443) and the TURN server ports (3478 UDP/TCP, 5349 for TLS TURN). The administrative interfaces (Grafana, Kibana/Loki, RabbitMQ management) are accessible only from a designated administrative network segment, not from the general office network.

**TLS enforcement:** All external connections require TLS 1.3 minimum. TLS 1.2 support can be enabled for specific legacy integration partners if needed but requires explicit security review. HTTP-to-HTTPS redirect is enforced at Nginx. HSTS headers are set on all responses.

**JWT validation at the gateway:** The API Gateway module validates every request's JWT against the IAM domain's public key before the request reaches any domain service. Requests without a valid, non-expired, non-revoked JWT are rejected at the gateway with 401 — they never reach the application layer. Session revocation (user suspended, session explicitly terminated) takes effect within the JWT's remaining validity window (maximum 30 minutes by default).

**Schema-level database isolation:** Each domain's application database role has been granted SELECT/INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE only on its own schema. No application role can read or write another domain's schema. This is enforced at the PostgreSQL role level — it cannot be bypassed by application code. The database superuser (used only for migrations and emergency operations) is never used by application processes.

### 10.2 Secrets Management

**Phase 1:** Secrets injected as environment variables at container startup from a deployment script that reads from an encrypted secrets file. The secrets file is never committed to version control. This is adequate for Phase 1 but limits secret rotation (requires container restart).

**Phase 2:** HashiCorp Vault deployed as the secrets management infrastructure. All secrets (database credentials, JWT signing keys, MinIO access keys, integration tokens) stored in Vault. Application containers use the Vault agent sidecar to retrieve secrets at startup and receive notifications on secret rotation. Secret rotation no longer requires container restarts.

**Key rotation schedule:** JWT signing keys: 90-day rotation. Database passwords: 180-day rotation. MinIO access keys: 90-day rotation. All rotations are scripted and tested in staging before production execution.

### 10.3 Encryption at Rest

**PostgreSQL data at rest:** Filesystem-level encryption using LUKS (Linux Unified Key Setup) on the database volumes. This protects against physical disk theft — the database data is unreadable without the filesystem decryption key.

**MinIO object storage:** Server-side encryption (SSE) using MinIO's built-in SSE-S3 mode. Objects are encrypted with AES-256. Encryption keys are managed through Vault (Phase 2) or through MinIO's internal key management (Phase 1). The `/recordings` bucket uses a distinct encryption key from other buckets, providing an additional isolation layer for sensitive recordings.

**Redis:** Redis does not natively encrypt stored data. Sensitive data in Redis (session tokens, authorization decision cache) is encrypted at the application level before writing to Redis. The Redis instance's access is network-restricted to application service nodes only.

### 10.4 Internal Service Trust

Within the server tier (application servers to database servers), communication occurs over a trusted private VLAN. Service-to-service communication within the monolith is in-process (no network). Service-to-service communication between extracted services (monolith to Real-time Gateway, monolith to GIS Service) uses HTTP over the private network with application-level authentication (service account JWT, not user JWT).

Service account JWTs are short-lived (4 hours) and are automatically refreshed by services using the IAM service account endpoint. A service account JWT compromise has a bounded exploitation window.

### 10.5 Privileged Admin Controls

Administrative capabilities follow the SoD model defined in the RBAC architecture document. At the infrastructure level:

- Server SSH access: restricted to a dedicated bastion host, requiring key-based authentication. No password-based SSH. SSH access is logged via the bastion host's audit log and produces audit events.
- Database superuser: accessible only from the bastion host, requires key-based authentication, all sessions logged.
- Vault root token: generated during setup, split using Shamir's Secret Sharing (minimum 3-of-5 keyholders required to reconstruct), sealed after initial setup. Vault unsealing requires physical presence of keyholders.
- Nginx configuration changes: require deployment through the CI/CD pipeline (not direct file editing on the server), providing a review and audit trail for all routing configuration changes.

## 11. Reliability and Disaster Recovery

### 11.1 Failure Domains

The platform is designed so that no single component failure causes complete platform unavailability. Failure domains are isolated:

**Database failure:** PostgreSQL primary failure → Patroni automatic failover to hot standby within 10-30 seconds. Applications experience brief connection errors during failover; they retry with exponential backoff and reconnect to the new primary. Write operations during failover (the reconnection window) fail and must be retried by clients. Read operations continue from the read replica during primary failover.

**Application server failure:** One of two or three application server instances fails → Nginx detects the failed upstream (health check failure within 10 seconds) and routes all traffic to remaining instances. Remaining instances absorb the load (sized for this). Users experience no visible interruption if the failure does not affect their current connection.

**Real-time Gateway instance failure:** Connected WebSocket clients are disconnected. Clients reconnect automatically (reconnection logic with exponential backoff). State synchronization on reconnection delivers missed messages. Reconnection completes within 5-15 seconds for most clients. Active calls through the media subsystem are not affected — signaling reconnects through the remaining gateway instances.

**SFU instance failure:** Active call sessions on the failed instance are terminated. The SFU cluster runs at a utilization level that allows remaining instances to absorb new sessions. Affected users see a call termination event and can rejoin.

**Redis failure:** A single Redis cluster node failure is tolerated by the cluster (data is distributed across nodes; a node failure causes slot redistribution). If the majority of Redis nodes fail, the Real-time Gateway loses session state. Connected clients are disconnected. On reconnection, state is rebuilt from the Chat Domain's PostgreSQL (source of truth). Authorization decision cache misses cause slightly elevated latency (the Authorization domain is queried directly) but no functional failure.

**Event bus failure:** RabbitMQ cluster node failure → queues remain available on surviving nodes (mirrored queues). New messages are delivered. In-flight messages on the failed node may be lost if not yet mirrored. At-least-once delivery semantics mean producers retry unconfirmed messages. Consumer processing of duplicate messages is idempotent (designed for this).

### 11.2 Mission-Critical Module Priority

In a disaster recovery scenario (full data center failure, catastrophic hardware failure requiring restoration from backup), services are restored in priority order:

**Priority 1 — Core Operations Foundation (< 2 hours RTO):**
IAM, Authorization, OrgManagement, EDMS, TaskManagement. These are the operational backbone. Emergency management decisions, document approvals, and task execution cannot function without them.

**Priority 2 — Communication (< 4 hours RTO):**
Real-time Gateway, Chat Domain, Notification. Internal coordination depends on communication infrastructure.

**Priority 3 — GIS and Analytics (< 8 hours RTO):**
GIS Service, spatial map visualization, incident registry. Critical for active emergency response but personnel can coordinate through Priority 1 and 2 services meanwhile.

**Priority 4 — Conference and Media (< 12 hours RTO):**
Media Subsystem, conferencing. Important but personnel can use Priority 2 text communication.

**Priority 5 — Analytics and AI (< 24 hours RTO):**
Analytical dashboards, AI risk layers. These are informational intelligence tools; their absence does not prevent emergency response.

### 11.3 Degraded Mode Operation

The platform must function in degraded mode when specific components are unavailable. Designed degraded states:

**Without real-time connection:** Chat messages are queued and delivered on reconnection. Task notifications are delivered via email fallback. Document approvals proceed asynchronously. The platform remains fully functional for all transactional operations.

**Without GIS service:** All EDMS, TaskManagement, and Communication functions continue. The GIS map views are unavailable. Incident reports can still be filed as structured text forms (without map visualization). GIS-specific analytics are unavailable.

**Without AI prediction service:** Operational and analytical functions continue unchanged. The AI risk layers disappear from the map. Analysts use historical risk data and their own expertise for risk assessment. This is the pre-AI-platform mode of operation — still fully operational.

**Without the analytical store:** Operational data entry, document workflows, and task management continue. Dashboard views that query the analytical store fall back to showing stale cached data with a staleness indicator. Real-time operational data (current task status, current incident locations) is unaffected.

## 12. Observability and Operations Model

### 12.1 Three-Layer Observability

**Layer 1 — Metrics (Prometheus + Grafana):**
Every service exposes a `/metrics` endpoint in Prometheus format. Metrics are scraped every 15 seconds. Key metrics per service category:

- Monolith: request rate, latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99), error rate, active database connections, queue depths
- Real-time Gateway: active WebSocket connections, message delivery rate, fan-out latency, connection establishment failures
- GIS Service: tile cache hit rate, spatial query latency, pipeline run duration, ingestion lag
- Media Subsystem: active SFU sessions, participant count, TURN relay usage, recording pipeline lag
- Infrastructure: PostgreSQL connection count, replication lag, database query duration, disk I/O, Redis memory usage, RabbitMQ queue depth and consumer count, MinIO storage utilization

Grafana dashboards organized by: service health overview, database performance, communication infrastructure, GIS performance, and operational capacity.

**Layer 2 — Logs (Loki + Promtail + Grafana):**
All services emit structured JSON logs. Promtail agents on every node ship logs to Loki. Logs include correlation IDs, enabling full-chain trace reconstruction from a single log query.

Log levels: ERROR (always), WARN (always), INFO (business events, service starts/stops, configuration changes), DEBUG (disabled in production by default, enableable per-service without restart via configuration flag).

Application logs and security audit logs are in separate log streams. Security audit logs have a longer retention policy and are protected from modification by application-level log users.

**Layer 3 — Traces (Jaeger):**
Distributed tracing for cross-service request flows. Correlation IDs are propagated as trace context through HTTP headers (W3C Trace Context standard). When a user reports a slow operation, the trace for that request shows exactly which service and database query consumed the latency.

Tracing is sampled at 10% in production (100% of error traces are always captured). Sampling rate is configurable without restart.

### 12.2 Audit vs. Technical Logs

**Technical logs** (application logs, system logs) answer: "What did the software do?" Retention: 90 days. Accessible by: system administrators, DevOps engineers. Used for: debugging, performance investigation, incident root cause analysis.

**Security audit logs** (events from the Audit domain) answer: "What did users do with institutional data?" Retention: 7 years minimum. Accessible by: authorized auditors only. Used for: compliance, investigation, access governance review.

These two streams must never be mixed. Technical logs are subject to log rotation and truncation. Audit logs are append-only and never truncated within their retention window.

### 12.3 Alerting Strategy

Alerting is organized by severity:

**P1 — Immediate response (on-call pager):** Service completely unavailable (health check failures on all instances), database primary failure, disk usage > 90%, security audit log delivery failure, active emergency response task system unavailable.

**P2 — Within-hour response (alert notification to operations team):** Single service instance failure (other instances absorbing load), database replication lag > 30 seconds, disk usage > 75%, message queue depth > 10,000 (pipeline backup), tile cache hit rate < 50% (GIS performance degradation).

**P3 — Same-day review (operations dashboard):** Elevated error rates (> 1% of requests), slow query threshold exceeded (> 500ms p95), ML prediction pipeline failed to run on schedule, data ingestion pipeline missed update.

Alert thresholds are calibrated in staging and adjusted based on production baselines. Alerting is not configured during the first month of production — the first month establishes baselines; alert thresholds are set based on observed normal operating ranges, not theoretical values.

## 13. Performance and Scaling Strategy

### 13.1 Where Each Component Scales and How

**EDMS and Task Management (I/O bound, scales horizontally):**
Document and task operations are mostly database reads and writes. Bottleneck: PostgreSQL connection count and query throughput. Horizontal scaling: add monolith application instances behind the load balancer (stateless application layer). Database scaling: add read replicas for read-heavy dashboard queries; increase connection pool size via PgBouncer; partition large tables (documents by year, tasks by department) when individual table size exceeds efficient index scan range.

First bottleneck: the shared PostgreSQL database. When the monolith has scaled to 3+ instances and database CPU is the bottleneck, the schema-separated model enables per-domain database extraction.

**Chat and Messaging (I/O and connection-bound, scales horizontally):**
Message persistence is database I/O. Fan-out is event bus throughput. Connection management is Real-time Gateway capacity. Each layer scales independently. At CoESCD scale, the bottleneck is unlikely to be the infrastructure — it is more likely to be the PostgreSQL write throughput on the chat schema during peak message periods.

First bottleneck: chat schema database write throughput. Mitigation: extract the chat schema to a dedicated PostgreSQL instance. This is the first likely database extraction in Phase 3.

**Conferencing and Media (CPU-bound, scales horizontally):**
SFU CPU is the bottleneck. Each 720p video stream from a participant requires approximately 25-50ms of SFU forwarding CPU per destination participant. Scaling: add SFU instances to the cluster; distribute sessions across instances through the call orchestrator's load balancing. SFU instances are sized specifically for media workloads (high CPU, moderate memory, high network bandwidth) — different hardware profile from application servers.

**GIS Queries (I/O and CPU, scales with read replicas):**
Spatial queries are CPU-intensive (spatial functions) and I/O-intensive (large geometry datasets). Bottleneck: PostGIS database CPU for complex spatial queries, tile server CPU for tile generation under cold-cache conditions. Scaling: PostGIS read replicas for spatial query load; pre-seeded tile cache to eliminate tile generation from the critical path for common regions.

**Analytics (CPU and I/O, scales with dedicated hardware):**
Heavy analytical queries (multi-year aggregations, cross-dataset correlations) are CPU and I/O intensive. TimescaleDB's continuous aggregates pre-compute the most common queries. Bottleneck: when queries become complex enough to exceed continuous aggregate coverage. Migration path to ClickHouse provides an order-of-magnitude improvement for complex analytical workloads without changing the query interface.

**AI/ML Training (CPU/GPU-bound, scales with dedicated hardware):**
Training workloads are isolated from the production serving path. They run on a dedicated ML server during off-peak hours. Scaling: add compute to the ML server (more CPU cores or GPU if deep learning models are introduced); parallelize training across cores using Dask. Training is a batch process — its throughput requirement is "complete training in < 8 hours," not "handle concurrent requests."

**AI/ML Inference (latency-sensitive, scales with replicas):**
Risk score inference is a batch process (run once daily); seismic impact inference is triggered by events (low frequency). Neither requires horizontal scaling at CoESCD's operational scale. The inference endpoint is sized for single-request latency (< 5 seconds for a full watershed risk batch run), not for concurrent request throughput.

### 13.2 Anticipated Bottleneck Sequence

Based on growth patterns typical for government internal platforms of this type, the anticipated sequence of first bottlenecks as the platform grows:

1. **Tile cache miss rate** (early, after initial deployment when the cache is cold): Resolved by pre-seeding base layer tiles.
2. **PostgreSQL connection count** (as monolith instances scale): Resolved by PgBouncer tuning.
3. **EDMS document search** (as the document corpus grows): Resolved by OpenSearch query optimization and index tuning.
4. **Chat schema write throughput** (as messaging usage grows): Resolved by chat schema database extraction.
5. **GIS spatial query CPU** (as GIS dataset density increases): Resolved by PostGIS read replica additions and query optimization.
6. **Analytics query response time** (as analytical complexity grows): Resolved by TimescaleDB continuous aggregate additions; later by ClickHouse migration.

None of these bottlenecks require architectural change — they are resolved through infrastructure scaling and tuning within the designed architecture.

## 14. MVP vs Full Platform Scope

### 14.1 MVP Definition

The Minimum Viable Platform is the smallest deployable subset that delivers genuine operational value — the set of capabilities without which the platform would not be adopted or would not replace current manual processes.

**In MVP (Phase 0 + Phase 1 output):**
- User authentication (local username/password)
- Organizational hierarchy management (departments, positions, reporting lines)
- Role-based access control (full three-layer model, because simplifying it creates technical debt that is expensive to correct later)
- User management (create, assign to positions, manage)
- EDMS core: document creation, registration, routing, sequential approval, basic execution assignment, document history
- Task management core: manual task creation, hierarchical assignment, status tracking, deadline monitoring, basic escalation
- File management: upload, download, attachment to documents and tasks, classification enforcement
- Notification: in-app and email for document routing and task assignment events
- Audit: complete audit log for all MVP functions
- Administration: user, role, department management

**Not in MVP (designed but not built):**
- Chat and real-time communication
- Audio/video conferencing
- GIS and spatial data
- Analytics dashboards
- AI/ML forecasting
- Mobile applications

**Designed now but built later (non-negotiable design decisions made in MVP):**
- SSO readiness: IAM's authentication abstraction is implemented in MVP even though SSO integration is not. The token model and validation contract are stable.
- Classification model: The five-level classification scheme is fully implemented in MVP. Retrofitting classification onto an unclassified data model is one of the most expensive rework operations in enterprise software.
- Audit schema: The audit event format is finalized and all MVP domains emit to it from day one. An audit schema change after deployment requires retroactive migration of potentially millions of records.
- Domain boundaries and API contracts: The internal API contracts between domains are finalized in Phase 0 and followed strictly from the first line of code. A domain that starts with informal inter-module communication creates coupling debt that compounds.

### 14.2 Full Platform Definition

The full platform includes all capabilities described in the architecture documents:
- Complete EDMS with parallel workflows, digital signature readiness, workflow templates, advanced execution control
- Full task hierarchy, dependency support, cross-department task control, reporting
- Complete chat, direct messages, department channels, task-linked and document-linked channels
- Audio/video conferencing with recording, conference management, scheduled meetings
- Complete GIS platform: operational layers, hazard zones, spatial analytics, incident mapping
- Analytics: dashboards, trend analysis, scheduled reports, performance metrics
- AI/ML forecasting: flood, landslide, fire risk models; seismic impact estimation; anomaly detection
- Mobile application for field operators
- SSO integration with government identity infrastructure
- Advanced administration: delegated admin, capability audit, SoD monitoring

### 14.3 The Critical Design Discipline

The distinction between "built in MVP" and "built later" must not be confused with "designed in MVP" and "designed later." Capabilities that are built later must still be architecturally accommodated from day one:

The ML forecasting integration contract is designed in Phase 0 even though the first ML model is not deployed until Phase 5. The GIS service's PostGIS schema includes the geometry columns and spatial indexes for AI risk layers even in Phase 1, because retrofitting spatial columns requires rebuilding indexes on large tables. The digital signature metadata fields in the EDMS signing step exist from the first document schema, populated with placeholder values until the signature service is available.

## 15. Phased Implementation Roadmap

### Phase 0 — Architecture, Governance, and Platform Foundation

**Duration:** 8-10 weeks

**Scope:**
This phase produces no user-facing features. It produces the infrastructure and governance foundation without which every subsequent phase builds on uncertain ground.

- Finalize and approve all domain boundaries, internal API contracts, and cross-domain event schemas. Document in ADRs (Architecture Decision Records) — the team must be able to explain every boundary decision.
- Establish the PostgreSQL multi-schema database with role grants enforced for each domain schema.
- Deploy the infrastructure: PostgreSQL (primary + standby via Patroni), Redis cluster, RabbitMQ cluster, MinIO distributed mode, OpenSearch cluster, Nginx.
- Deploy the observability stack: Prometheus, Grafana, Loki, Jaeger, Alertmanager.
- Implement the shared cross-cutting library: JWT parsing and validation, Authorization domain client (wrapping the `can()` API), audit event emitter, correlation ID propagation middleware.
- Establish the CI/CD pipeline: code quality gates, automated tests, container build, staged deployment to test and staging environments.
- Implement configuration management: environment separation, secrets handling.
- Finalize the audit event schema: every domain will emit to this schema from their first line of code.
- Implement the IAM domain's authentication abstraction (pluggable provider interface) and basic username/password authentication.

**Why this phase exists:** The decisions made in Phase 0 are the most expensive to change later. A schema naming convention change in Phase 5 costs days. A domain boundary reconsideration in Phase 3 costs weeks. An audit schema change after 2 million records costs months. The investment in Phase 0 is the highest-ROI engineering work in the roadmap.

**Dependencies:** None (this is the foundation).

**Outcome:** A running infrastructure with all shared services operational, a deployable base application with IAM, and a development environment that all team members can run locally.

---

### Phase 1 — Core Operational Foundation

**Duration:** 12-14 weeks

**Scope:**
The organizational backbone and the administrative governance layer. At the end of Phase 1, the platform can be used to manage the organization's users, roles, and hierarchy — and the access model that governs everything else is in production.

- OrgManagement: full department hierarchy, position management, reporting lines, command chain graph.
- UserManagement: user profiles, position occupancy, delegation model, acting assignments.
- Authorization: full three-layer model (RBAC + hierarchical + classification), `can()` API, SoD rule enforcement, access decision caching.
- FileManagement: upload, download, metadata, classification, versioning, virus scanning integration, MinIO integration, pre-signed URL generation.
- Notification: in-app notification delivery via Real-time Gateway (basic), email delivery via SMTP.
- Audit domain: full event storage, tamper detection (hash chaining), administrative query interface for auditors.
- Administration panel: user lifecycle management, role/position/department management, audit log review.
- Real-time Gateway: Phase 1 deployment (basic WebSocket connection management for in-app notifications — chat is Phase 3 but the gateway infrastructure is deployed now to serve notifications).

**Why this phase:** Every subsequent phase depends on the authorization model, the organizational hierarchy, and file management. A document cannot be routed without knowing positions. A task cannot be assigned without the command chain. Deploying these in Phase 1 means all subsequent phases build on a proven foundation.

**Dependencies:** Phase 0 complete.

**Outcome:** Administrators can fully configure the organization. Users can log in and access the platform. File uploads work. The access model is enforced. The audit trail is running.

### Phase 2 — EDMS and Task Execution

**Duration:** 16-20 weeks

**Scope:**
The formal documentary and task execution workflows. This is the phase where the platform begins delivering primary business value — replacing paper-based document routing and informal task assignment.

- EDMS complete: document types, document registration and numbering, sequential and parallel workflow templates, approval chains by position, execution assignments, deadline control, execution tracking, document history, classification enforcement, document search and retrieval, document-file attachment lifecycle.
- Task Management complete: manual task creation, hierarchical assignment with command chain enforcement, primary and co-executor model, task lifecycle (all states), subtask decomposition (2 levels), deadline monitoring and escalation logic, progress reporting, completion verification, executor assignment from EDMS resolutions, bidirectional EDMS-TaskManagement synchronization.

**Why this phase:** EDMS and TaskManagement are the primary operational systems for CoESCD's daily workflows. Getting these right is the platform's core value proposition. This phase is the longest because the workflow complexity is real — sequential, parallel, delegation, escalation, and execution control are not trivially implemented correctly.

**Dependencies:** Phase 1 complete. Organizational hierarchy must be populated with real data before workflows can be configured and tested.

**Outcome:** The organization can process incoming correspondence, issue orders, route documents for approval, assign execution tasks, and track completion — entirely within the platform.

### Phase 3 — Real-time Communication

**Duration:** 12-14 weeks

**Scope:**
Internal communication infrastructure. At the end of Phase 3, the platform supports secure internal messaging, presence, audio/video calls, and task/document-linked discussion channels.

- Chat Domain complete: direct messages, department channels, group channels, task-linked channels, document-linked channels, message history, read/delivery receipts, file sharing in chat, message retention policy enforcement.
- Real-time Gateway full deployment: complete event fan-out, presence system, typing indicators, multi-device handling.
- Conferencing: one-to-one calls, group calls, scheduled conferences, screen sharing, conference management.
- Media Subsystem deployment: Mediasoup SFU cluster, TURN/STUN server, recording pipeline, MinIO recording storage.
- Notification enhancement: chat message notifications for offline users, call invitation delivery.
- Communication-EDMS integration: document-linked channels created with document workflow activation.
- Communication-TaskManagement integration: task-linked channels created with task creation.

**Why this phase after EDMS and Tasks:** The formal workflows (EDMS, Tasks) must be in production and generating real organizational usage before the communication layer is added. The task-linked and document-linked channels only have value when tasks and documents are actively being created. Deploying communication before the primary workflows are in production means the channels are empty and the feature is underutilized.

**Dependencies:** Phase 2 complete. Real-time Gateway infrastructure was deployed in Phase 1; full feature deployment occurs here.

**Outcome:** The platform is the primary internal communication tool. Document routing conversations happen in the platform. Task execution discussions happen in task-linked channels. Audio/video calls replace external communication tools for internal coordination.

---

### Phase 4 — GIS and Operational Analytics

**Duration:** 14-16 weeks

**Scope:**
Spatial intelligence and analytical reporting. At the end of Phase 4, the platform provides operational situational awareness through GIS maps, incident analytics, and reporting.

- GIS Service deployment: PostGIS database, spatial dataset management, base layer catalog, hazard zone layers, infrastructure layers.
- Incident registry and GIS data collection forms for field analysts.
- Map rendering: Martin (vector tiles), GeoServer (raster tiles), Nginx tile caching, MBTiles base map pre-seeding.
- Spatial analytics: proximity queries, area containment queries, spatial enrichment pipeline.
- Data ingestion pipelines: file-based batch ingestion (GeoJSON, Shapefile, KML, GeoTIFF), external API integration for meteorological and seismic feeds, Airflow pipeline orchestration.
- TimescaleDB analytical store: incident time-series, continuous aggregates, dashboard query targets.
- Analytics platform: report templates, on-demand and scheduled report generation, analytical dashboards (incident trends, regional statistics, response performance).
- GIS-TaskManagement integration: active task locations visualized on the operational map.
- GIS-Notification integration: spatial proximity alerts for new incidents.
- Export functionality: GeoJSON, Shapefile, PDF map exports, Excel/CSV analytical reports.

**Why this phase after communication:** GIS ingestion pipelines depend on incident reporting forms that are now populated with real incident data (from Phase 2's task execution and Phase 3's communication-supported field coordination). Analytical dashboards have meaningful data to display only after the platform has been in operational use for several months. Deploying analytics before the platform has operational data produces empty dashboards that discourage adoption.

**Dependencies:** Phase 3 complete. A minimum of 3-6 months of operational data from Phase 2 significantly improves the analytical value at Phase 4 launch.

**Outcome:** Leadership and analysts have real-time situational awareness through operational maps. Incident trends are visible in dashboards. Reports are generated automatically. The platform is the primary analytical tool for emergency management intelligence.

---

### Phase 5 — AI/ML Forecasting

**Duration:** 16-20 weeks

**Scope:**
Predictive intelligence layer. At the end of Phase 5, the platform produces validated AI-based risk forecasts for flood, landslide, and fire hazards, integrated into the operational GIS map.

- Historical data audit and quality assessment (prerequisite: minimum 2-3 years of quality incident data from Phase 4 operations, supplemented by historical records migration).
- Feature store development: feature catalog, feature computation pipelines (7-day precipitation, soil moisture, NDVI, slope attributes, historical incident density).
- Flood risk model: training on historical flood events + meteorological features; evaluation; staging deployment; analyst validation workflow.
- Landslide risk model: training on historical landslide inventory + terrain + precipitation features; evaluation; staging.
- Fire risk model: training on historical fire events + KBDI + NDVI features; evaluation; staging.
- Seismic impact estimation model: training on historical earthquake events + building inventory + population data.
- AI/ML integration with GIS: draft layer publication workflow, analyst review interface, OPERATIONAL layer promotion, temporal layer navigation.
- Anomaly detection: CUSUM-based incident rate anomaly flagging.
- Model monitoring: prediction accuracy tracking, model drift detection, retraining triggers.
- MLflow model registry deployment.

**Why this phase last:** AI models require historical data to be trained on. The quality of forecasting is directly proportional to the quality and completeness of the historical incident catalog. Deploying AI in Phase 2 would produce models trained on limited data, generating unreliable forecasts that erode analyst trust. By Phase 5, the platform has 2-3 years of structured, validated incident data — the minimum for producing models with meaningful operational value.

**Dependencies:** Phase 4 complete. Minimum 12-24 months of operational incident data. Historical incident catalog migration completed (importing pre-platform paper records into the structured format).

**Outcome:** The platform generates monthly risk forecasts for major hazard types. Risk layers are reviewed by senior analysts, approved, and published to the operational map. Leadership has AI-supported evidence for seasonal preparedness decisions.

---

### Phase 6 — Hardening, Optimization, and Scale-up

**Duration:** Ongoing, starting 3 months after Phase 5 deployment

**Scope:**
This phase has no completion date — it is the continuous improvement phase.

- SSO integration: LDAP/AD integration if CoESCD has an existing IT directory; SAML federation if government SSO infrastructure exists.
- Mobile application: read-focused mobile interface for field operators (task viewing, incident reporting from mobile).
- Performance optimization based on production usage data: query optimization, cache tuning, index additions, materialized view additions for observed query patterns.
- Advanced audit features: SIEM export, cross-domain audit trail visualization for investigators.
- Delegated administration: department-level admin capabilities.
- Workflow template library: pre-configured workflow templates for common document types, tested and validated through operational use.
- Advanced ML: model retraining on larger historical dataset, additional hazard types (avalanche), scenario modeling capabilities.
- Digital signature integration: integration with government digital signature infrastructure when available.
- Platform extraction decisions: based on 12+ months of production metrics, decide which domains (if any) have demonstrated sufficient need for independent scaling to justify extraction.

## 17. Key Risks and Anti-Patterns

### Risk 1: Premature microservice decomposition

**The anti-pattern:** Under pressure from stakeholders who have heard about microservices, or from engineers who prefer distributed systems, the team extracts domains into separate services before the domain boundaries are proven in production and before the operational maturity exists to manage them.

**The consequence:** The system becomes operationally complex before it is feature-complete. Debugging distributed failures consumes engineering time that should go to features. The domain boundaries, established before production evidence, turn out to be wrong — and now they are network boundaries that are expensive to change.

**The mitigation:** Extract a domain only when it demonstrates a specific, measurable need: a specific domain's scaling requirement cannot be met by the shared database, or a specific domain needs a different deployment cadence from the rest. Evidence-based extraction only. Every extraction proposal must include: the specific production metric that requires extraction, the operational cost of managing an additional service, and the alternative approaches considered and rejected.

---

### Risk 2: Authorization model drift

**The anti-pattern:** Each domain, under time pressure, implements shortcuts in its own access control logic. "We'll just check if the user's department ID matches the document's department ID — that's basically the same thing." Over time, each domain has a slightly different implementation of authorization rules. Some enforce classification; some don't. Some check the three-layer model; some only check roles.

**The consequence:** Authorization behavior becomes inconsistent and unpredictable. Security audits reveal gaps that are expensive to trace through distributed implementation. A permission change in the Authorization domain does not propagate to domains that bypassed it.

**The mitigation:** Architecture review for any code that makes an access control decision without calling the Authorization domain's `can()` API is a blocking review comment. This is enforced as a code quality gate in CI: a static analysis rule flags any branch in the codebase that performs a permission check without going through the Authorization module's interface.

---

### Risk 3: Data governance neglect in Phase 4

**The anti-pattern:** The GIS data platform is deployed with rich ingestion capabilities but without assigning data steward responsibilities to specific positions. Datasets accumulate without quality review. Stale layers remain on the operational map. The ML training data includes unvalidated records that corrupt model performance.

**The consequence:** Analyst trust in the GIS platform collapses when they find outdated hazard zone boundaries, incorrectly geocoded incident records, and ML risk layers that contradict observable field conditions.

**The mitigation:** Phase 4 includes a governance configuration step (not just technical deployment): every dataset in the catalog has a named data steward, assigned from the organization's positions, before the dataset goes operational. The data steward acceptance is a gating criterion for Phase 4 go-live.

---

### Risk 4: AI deployed before data maturity

**The anti-pattern:** Leadership pressure to demonstrate "AI capabilities" causes Phase 5 to be pulled forward before the incident data quality and volume are sufficient for meaningful model training.

**The consequence:** Poorly trained models produce high-confidence predictions that are frequently wrong. Analysts quickly learn not to trust the risk layers. The AI forecasting capability is abandoned, and the investment is written off.

**The mitigation:** Phase 5 is gated on a data maturity assessment. The minimum criteria are: 24 months of structured incident data for the primary hazard types, data quality metrics (completeness > 90%, geocoding accuracy > 95%) for the training dataset, and successful back-testing of the candidate models against at least two historical events that were not in the training set. If the data is not mature enough, Phase 5 is delayed — not the data quality standards lowered.

---

### Risk 5: Audit gaps during the transition from manual processes

**The anti-pattern:** During the phased rollout, some processes are managed in the new platform while others continue in paper/email/phone workflows. The audit trail in the platform is complete for platform-based actions but missing the external actions that influenced platform state (a phone call that preceded a document approval, a paper directive that triggered a task).

**The consequence:** The audit trail is incomplete and therefore not reliable as a governance tool. Investigations that require a complete chain of events cannot be fully reconstructed.

**The mitigation:** For each phase's scope, the organizational rollout plan includes explicit guidance on which processes must be performed in the platform from the phase's go-live date. Parallel operation (doing the same process in both the platform and the old method) is a transition period only, with a defined end date. After the end date, the platform workflow is the only authorized workflow for in-scope processes.

---

### Risk 6: Media infrastructure complexity overwhelming the operations team

**The anti-pattern:** The Media Subsystem (SFU, TURN, recording pipeline) is deployed by the engineering team and then handed to an operations team that has no WebRTC or media server experience. Media quality issues (call drops, audio artifacts, recording failures) are misdiagnosed as application bugs rather than network or media server configuration issues.

**The mitigation:** The Media Subsystem requires dedicated operational knowledge. The team member responsible for Phase 3's Media Subsystem deployment must document operational procedures (how to diagnose a degraded SFU, how to interpret RTCP metrics, what network conditions cause TURN relay usage spikes) before Phase 3 goes live. A runbook, not just a technical architecture document, is the Phase 3 delivery requirement for the media infrastructure.

---

### Risk 7: GIS tile performance under cold cache

**The anti-pattern:** The GIS service is deployed to production without pre-seeding the tile cache. The first day of production, every user request is a tile cache miss — the tile server generates tiles on demand for every request. The GIS map appears slow. Users report poor performance. Management questions the GIS implementation quality.

**The mitigation:** Pre-seeding the base layer tiles (zoom levels 0-12 for all of Tajikistan) is a deployment step, not an optimization. It runs during the Phase 4 deployment process before the service is opened to users. Pre-seeding takes several hours; it runs as part of the deployment pipeline, not as a post-go-live task.

---

### Risk 8: The EDMS workflow engine as a bottleneck for organizational change

**The anti-pattern:** EDMS workflow templates are hardcoded by developers rather than configured by administrators. When the organization restructures (a new department is added, an approval chain is changed by leadership directive), the change requires a software deployment cycle (development → test → staging → production) rather than an administrative configuration change.

**The consequence:** The EDMS becomes an organizational obstacle rather than an enabler. Departments work around the system because it cannot keep up with organizational reality.

**The mitigation:** Phase 2's EDMS implementation must include the workflow template configuration interface for administrators from day one. Not as a Phase 3 enhancement — from the moment the first workflow template is created. The first workflow template should be created by an administrator using the template interface, not hardcoded by a developer, to validate that the interface is genuinely usable for non-technical administrators.

## 18. Final Integrated Architecture Recommendation

The CoESCD Unified Digital Platform is designed as a coherent institutional intelligence system — not a collection of modules, not a feature backlog, not a technology showcase. Every architectural decision in this document, and in the six domain-level architecture documents it integrates, is made in service of one proposition: the Committee of Emergency Situations and Civil Defense must be able to govern, coordinate, and respond as a more effective institution because this platform exists.

The architecture achieves this through seven integrated design principles that are consistent across every layer of the system:

---

**Principle 1 — One organizational model governs everything.**
The department hierarchy, position structure, and command chains defined in OrgManagement are the single authority for every access decision, every routing choice, every escalation chain, and every notification target across the entire platform. There is no domain that maintains its own copy of who reports to whom. Organizational reality changes in one place and propagates immediately and consistently everywhere.

---

**Principle 2 — Authority is structurally enforced, not conventionally observed.**
The Authorization domain's `can()` API is not a suggestion. It is the mandatory gate before every sensitive operation in every domain. No shortcut, no "we'll add this later," no "just check the department ID for now." The access control model is complete and correctly enforced from the first day of production. Retrofitting access control after data is in production is not a technical task — it is a security incident.

---

**Principle 3 — Every significant institutional action is permanently recorded.**
The audit trail is not an afterthought. It is the first infrastructure deployed in Phase 0 and the last thing that should ever be turned off. A document approval, a task assignment, a risk layer publication, a conference recording download — all are permanent institutional records. The platform does not provide a mechanism for covering tracks. Auditability is not just a compliance checkbox; it is the institutional trust foundation that makes the platform's authority legitimate.

---

**Principle 4 — The platform degrades gracefully, never catastrophically.**
Every failure scenario — database failover, service instance crash, media server overload, connectivity degradation — has a defined degraded operating mode. Emergency response coordination can continue with reduced capability even when specific components are unavailable. The system's behavior under failure is a design specification, not a disaster scenario.

---

**Principle 5 — The architecture is phased, not rushed.**
The six-phase roadmap is not a concession to limited resources. It is the correct approach for a complex institutional platform. Each phase delivers genuine operational value. Each phase's completion is the evidence base for the next phase's design decisions. AI models trained on three years of platform data are categorically more valuable than AI models trained on six months of data. The phased roadmap is not a slower path — it is the path that produces a platform the organization actually adopts and uses.

---

**Principle 6 — Complexity is earned, not assumed.**
The modular monolith is not a temporary compromise awaiting "proper" microservices. It is the architecturally correct topology for this team, this organization, and this phase of platform maturity. Each component extracted to an independent service (the Real-time Gateway, the GIS Service, the Media Subsystem) was extracted because its runtime characteristics make shared deployment technically incorrect — not because microservices are philosophically superior. Future extractions will be driven by production evidence, not by architectural fashion.

---

**Principle 7 — The platform serves the institution, not the other way around.**
Technology is the servant, not the master. Document workflows are configured to match how CoESCD actually processes documents — not to match how a generic workflow engine works. The organizational hierarchy is modeled to reflect how authority actually flows in a government emergency management institution — not to fit a generic RBAC framework. GIS hazard models are trained on Tajikistan's specific terrain, hydrology, and hazard history — not on generic emergency management datasets. At every level of the architecture, the platform's design follows institutional reality. Institutional reality is not expected to follow the platform's design.

---

This document, together with the six domain-level architecture documents it integrates, constitutes the complete architectural foundation for the CoESCD Unified Digital Platform. The foundation is now set. What follows is engineering.
