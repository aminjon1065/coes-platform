# CoESCD Unified Digital Platform — Architecture Foundation

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The Committee of Emergency Situations and Civil Defense (CoESCD) requires a unified digital platform that consolidates fragmented operational systems into a coherent, secure, and governable enterprise ecosystem. This is not a product build — it is the digital backbone of a national emergency management institution, and the architecture must reflect that gravity.

The recommended architecture is a **phased modular monolith transitioning toward a domain-partitioned distributed system**, deployed on-premises or in a sovereign government cloud. This approach delivers the fastest time-to-value, the lowest operational risk in the early phases, and a clear evolutionary path toward independently scalable domains as organizational maturity grows.

The platform is organized around **fifteen bounded domains**, united by a set of **mandatory cross-cutting platform capabilities** — identity, authorization, audit, notifications, file handling, search, and observability — that are treated as first-class infrastructure, not afterthoughts. Security is not a layer on top. It is woven into the data model, the access model, the communication model, and the audit model from the first line of architecture.

---

## 2. Platform Mission and Operating Context

**What this platform must accomplish:**

The CoESCD platform must serve as the operational nervous system of a government institution responsible for national emergency preparedness, disaster response, civil defense, and inter-agency coordination. It must function reliably during normal peacetime operations and must remain trustworthy and available precisely when crises occur — which is the moment the system is most critical and most likely to be under stress.

**Operating constraints that define the architecture:**

- **Sovereignty requirement.** Government data, particularly sensitive emergency and civil defense data, cannot reside in foreign commercial cloud infrastructure. The platform must be deployable in a government-controlled data center or national sovereign cloud with no dependency on third-party SaaS services for core functionality.

- **Offline resilience consideration.** In disaster scenarios, internet connectivity may be degraded. The architecture must anticipate degraded-mode operation for at minimum the communication and task management domains.

- **Hierarchical authority model.** The platform is used by a government organization with strict chains of command. The data access model, task routing, document approval chains, and communication permissions must reflect real organizational authority — not flat SaaS-style role models.

- **Regulatory and audit obligations.** Government operations require comprehensive, tamper-evident audit trails. Every significant action — document approval, task assignment, access to sensitive data, configuration changes — must be logged with chain-of-custody integrity.

- **Heterogeneous user base.** Users range from the Chairman and senior officials who need high-level dashboards and approval workflows, to field analysts who need GIS tools and incident data entry, to clerks who manage document correspondence, to administrators who govern the system. The platform must serve all of these roles with appropriate interfaces and access.

- **Phased delivery reality.** This platform will not be delivered in one release. It must be designed so that each phase delivers standalone business value, and no early phase decision creates architectural debt that blocks a later phase.

## 3. Architectural Drivers

Architectural drivers are the non-negotiable forces that constrain and shape every design decision. Understanding them explicitly prevents future decisions from optimizing for the wrong things.

**Quality attributes in priority order for CoESCD:**

| Priority | Quality Attribute | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Security** | Sensitive government data, civil defense information, access control |
| 2 | **Auditability** | Legal and institutional obligation for all government operations |
| 3 | **Reliability** | Must function during emergencies — this is not a business productivity tool |
| 4 | **Correctness** | Approval chains, task authority, and document routing must be exactly right |
| 5 | **Maintainability** | Long-lived government system — must be maintainable by successor teams |
| 6 | **Usability** | Adoption depends on it — government users have alternatives (paper) |
| 7 | **Performance** | Acceptable latency within LAN/government network — not public-internet SLOs |
| 8 | **Scalability** | Important but not the primary driver — this is not a consumer platform |

**Functional constraints that shape structure:**

- Strict hierarchical access control that mirrors organizational command chains
- Document workflows with multi-party approval, routing, and execution tracking
- Real-time communication with media (audio/video) that must remain internal
- Spatial data and GIS capabilities for hazard analysis and incident visualization
- Future AI/ML analytics that must be integrated without rebuilding the data pipeline
- Administration capabilities that must be delegatable within governed bounds

**Anti-drivers (things this platform is NOT):**

- Not a public-facing citizen portal
- Not a consumer mobile-first product
- Not a multi-tenant SaaS product
- Not a startup that needs infinite horizontal scaling from day one
- Not a system that can tolerate casual access control decisions

## 4. Recommended Architecture Style

**Recommendation: Modular Monolith as Foundation, with Explicit Domain Boundaries Designed for Future Partitioning**

More precisely: a **domain-partitioned backend monolith** with well-enforced internal module boundaries, a **dedicated real-time service** extracted from day one, and a **pluggable GIS/analytics plane** that operates semi-independently from the transactional core.

This is not a compromise. It is the architecturally correct choice for this specific platform at this specific organizational maturity level.

**What this means concretely:**

- A single deployable backend application organized into strictly bounded internal modules (domains), each owning its own data access layer, its own service layer, and its own internal API contracts
- No shared database tables between modules — each module has its own database schema or database instance, accessed only through its own service layer
- A synchronous HTTP/REST internal API for inter-module communication within the monolith
- An internal event bus (initially an in-process event dispatcher, later promotable to a message broker) for asynchronous cross-domain events
- One or two services extracted from day one where the runtime requirements differ fundamentally: real-time communication (WebSocket/XMPP infrastructure), and optionally media handling (WebRTC signaling)
- A separately deployable GIS/spatial analytics module that can run alongside or independently
- A shared infrastructure layer for cross-cutting concerns: authentication token validation, audit log emission, notification dispatch, file storage access

## 5. Why This Architecture Style Fits CoESCD

**Why not full microservices from day one?**

Microservices are an organizational scaling pattern as much as a technical one. They require:
- Multiple independent teams, each owning a service
- Mature DevOps tooling: container orchestration, service discovery, distributed tracing, API gateways
- Sophisticated monitoring to diagnose distributed failures
- Network-level security between services
- Distributed transaction management where consistency is required

CoESCD is a government institution building its first unified platform. The development team will likely be one or two teams, not ten. The operational staff will be IT generalists, not platform engineers. Deploying a 15-service microservices system day one would mean:
- Most of the engineering effort goes to infrastructure, not features
- Debugging cross-service failures is disproportionately hard
- Deployment complexity creates fragility in a mission-critical context
- The domain boundaries are not yet proven in production — premature decomposition locks in the wrong seams

**Why not a traditional monolith?**

A traditional monolith with shared tables, implicit dependencies, and no enforced module boundaries would deliver faster initially but create compounding architectural debt:
- Any module's schema change can affect any other module
- Business logic becomes tangled across domains
- Teams cannot work independently
- Testing domain behavior requires the entire system
- Future partitioning (when justified) requires a rewrite, not an extraction

**Why the modular monolith is right:**

- Module boundaries are enforced architecturally, not just by convention
- Each domain owns its data — cross-domain data access happens through service APIs, not joins
- The deployment model is simple: one application server, one (or few) databases, well-understood operations
- As the team grows and specific domains prove they need independent scaling (e.g., GIS analytics, real-time communication), those domains can be extracted without touching the other domains — because the boundaries were designed correctly from the start
- Government operational teams can manage a monolith; they struggle with Kubernetes-native microservice clusters

**The real-time communication exception:**

WebSocket-based real-time communication (chat, presence, calls) has fundamentally different runtime characteristics than transactional request/response. It maintains long-lived connections, requires horizontal scaling with sticky sessions or shared state, and benefits from dedicated server infrastructure. This domain is extracted as a dedicated service from day one — not because the team is large enough to maintain two services, but because the runtime model demands it.

**The GIS/spatial analytics exception:**

Spatial data processing, map tile serving, and analytics pipelines have specialized infrastructure needs (PostGIS, tile servers, Python-based spatial processing libraries) that are best isolated from the transactional core. This is modeled as a separately deployable component that shares identity infrastructure with the main platform but manages its own data pipelines.

## 6. Domain Decomposition / Bounded Contexts

Each domain below is a bounded context — it owns its data, its business logic, and its integrations. Nothing outside the domain touches its database directly.

---

### Domain 1: Identity and Access Management (IAM)

**Responsibility:** The authoritative source of identity for all platform users and service clients. Issues and validates authentication tokens. Owns the session lifecycle. Is the foundation on which all other authorization decisions are made.

**What belongs inside:**
- User identity records (username, credentials, password policy enforcement)
- Authentication flows (username/password, future MFA, future SSO/SAML/OIDC)
- Token issuance and validation (JWT or opaque tokens with a validation endpoint)
- Session management
- Password reset and credential lifecycle
- Service account management for internal service-to-service auth
- Future SSO federation configuration

**What stays outside:**
- Organizational structure (which department a user belongs to — that is OrgManagement's concern)
- Roles and permissions (that is Authorization's concern)
- User profile data beyond identity (that is UserManagement's concern)

**Data ownership:** Credential store, session store, token store. These are the most sensitive records in the system and should be isolated in a dedicated schema or database with the strongest access controls.

**Integrations:**
- All other domains validate tokens against IAM
- OrgManagement pushes provisioning events when users are created or deactivated
- Audit domain receives authentication events (login, logout, failed login, password change)

**Critical design note:** IAM must be designed SSO-ready from day one even if SSO is not the initial deployment mode. This means the authentication flow must be abstracted behind an interface that can be backed by local credentials, LDAP, or a SAML/OIDC provider without changing how downstream domains consume identity. Token format and validation contract must be stable.

---

### Domain 2: Organization and Department Management

**Responsibility:** The authoritative model of the organizational hierarchy. Owns departments, positions, reporting lines, and command chains. Is the structural backbone that gives all authorization decisions their hierarchical meaning.

**What belongs inside:**
- Department hierarchy (departments, sub-departments, units, sections)
- Position definitions (Chairman, Deputy, Department Head, Analyst, Clerk, Operator)
- Position-to-department assignments
- Reporting line definitions
- Command chain rules (who can assign tasks to whom, who can approve whose documents)
- Organizational change history (when departments were restructured)
- Cross-department authority rules (officials with authority outside their direct chain)

**What stays outside:**
- Individual user identity (IAM)
- User profile details (UserManagement)
- Role permissions (Authorization)

**Data ownership:** The org chart. Immutable history of org changes. Position catalog.

**Integrations:**
- Authorization domain reads the org model to evaluate hierarchical permission rules
- TaskManagement reads command chains to validate task assignment authority
- DocumentManagement reads approval chain definitions from org positions
- UserManagement links users to positions in this domain

**Critical design note:** This domain must model the distinction between **organizational position** (a slot in the hierarchy, e.g., "Head of Fire Safety Department") and **user occupancy of that position** (the person currently filling it). Positions persist through personnel changes. Documents, tasks, and approvals route to positions — not to individual users — so workflows survive personnel rotation.

---

### Domain 3: User Management

**Responsibility:** The profile layer on top of identity. Manages the human-facing attributes of platform users — their names, contact info, avatars, preferences, and their link to organizational positions.

**What belongs inside:**
- User profile (full name, display name, avatar, contact information)
- Position assignment (user linked to one or more org positions)
- User preferences and settings
- User status (active, inactive, on leave, suspended)
- User onboarding and offboarding workflows
- Delegation rules (user A can act on behalf of position X temporarily)

**What stays outside:**
- Authentication credentials (IAM)
- Role permissions (Authorization)
- Department structure (OrgManagement)

**Data ownership:** User profile records and position assignments.

**Integrations:**
- IAM for identity linkage
- OrgManagement for position definitions
- Authorization to trigger permission recalculation on position changes
- Notification domain for user contact channels
- Communication domain for presence status

---

### Domain 4: Authorization

**Responsibility:** The runtime enforcement layer for all access control decisions. Evaluates whether a specific user, in their current context, is allowed to perform a specific action on a specific resource.

**What belongs inside:**
- Role definitions
- Permission definitions (fine-grained capabilities: "create document", "approve document in own department", "view hazard zone map")
- Role-to-permission assignments
- User-to-role assignments
- Hierarchical permission rules (e.g., a Deputy has all permissions of department heads below them)
- Confidentiality level access rules
- Context-aware authorization policies (e.g., a document can only be viewed by roles at or above its classification level)
- Authorization decision cache

**What stays outside:**
- User identity (IAM)
- Org hierarchy (OrgManagement — authorization reads it, does not own it)

**Data ownership:** Role definitions, permission definitions, assignment tables. The authorization policy model.

**Integrations:**
- All domains call authorization to validate actions before execution
- OrgManagement provides hierarchy data for rule evaluation
- IAM provides identity context
- Audit domain receives authorization decisions for sensitive actions

**Critical design note:** Authorization must expose a simple, fast, cacheable decision API: `can(userId, action, resourceType, resourceId?) → allow/deny`. The internal complexity of evaluating hierarchical roles, confidentiality levels, and positional authority is hidden behind this interface. All domains consume only this interface — they do not implement their own permission logic. This is the single most important architectural discipline in the platform.

---

### Domain 5: Document Management

**Responsibility:** Manages the lifecycle of all official government documents — their creation, classification, routing, approval, execution, archiving, and history. This is the digital equivalent of the institution's official paper correspondence system.

**What belongs inside:**
- Document types (incoming, outgoing, internal, regulatory, order, resolution, correspondence)
- Document drafting and templating
- Classification and confidentiality tagging
- Routing rules and approval chains
- Approval workflow engine (multi-party, sequential, parallel, conditional)
- Execution control (assigned tasks, deadlines, completion tracking)
- Document versioning and revision history
- Document registry (incoming numbers, outgoing numbers)
- Archiving and retention rules
- Document search and filtering

**What stays outside:**
- The files attached to documents (FileManagement owns the file bytes)
- Task assignment that results from a document execution order (TaskManagement owns those tasks, created as a result of document commands)
- User identity and permissions (consumed from IAM and Authorization)

**Data ownership:** Document records, metadata, routing history, approval decisions, execution records. Document numbers. Retention schedules.

**Integrations:**
- FileManagement for attached files
- TaskManagement to create execution tasks from document orders
- OrgManagement and Authorization for routing chain resolution and access control
- Notification domain to alert approvers and recipients
- Audit domain for all document state changes

**Critical design note:** The document approval chain must be driven by **positions**, not users. When an approval chain is configured for "Head of Department X → Deputy Chairman → Chairman", it routes to whoever currently occupies those positions. This is how government document systems work in practice and it is a non-negotiable organizational requirement.

---

### Domain 6: Task Management

**Responsibility:** Manages work items assigned within the organization — their creation, delegation, progress tracking, deadline management, and accountability chain.

**What belongs inside:**
- Task types and categories
- Task creation and assignment (with authority validation)
- Hierarchical task delegation (task → subtask chains)
- Task statuses and workflow transitions
- Deadlines, urgency levels, and escalation rules
- Task comments and activity log
- Task attachments (file references)
- Execution tracking and reporting
- Task boards and views

**What stays outside:**
- Document-driven tasks that originate from document execution orders (DocumentManagement creates them, TaskManagement owns them after creation)
- Organizational authority rules (read from OrgManagement/Authorization — not replicated here)
- File storage (FileManagement)

**Data ownership:** Task records, assignment records, status history, comment threads.

**Integrations:**
- OrgManagement and Authorization to validate that assigner has authority over assignee
- DocumentManagement: receives task creation events from execution orders
- FileManagement for task attachments
- Notification for deadline reminders and assignment alerts
- Communication: tasks can be discussed in linked chat threads
- Audit for assignment and completion events

**Critical design note:** The platform must enforce that a user cannot assign tasks to a position outside their command authority. This validation must be authoritative — not advisory — and must be backed by the organizational hierarchy model in OrgManagement, not maintained locally in TaskManagement.

---

### Domain 7: Communication

**Responsibility:** Provides secure internal messaging — text-based chat, presence, one-to-one and group conversations, and structured collaboration channels. This is the internal messaging backbone of the platform.

**What belongs inside:**
- Direct messages (one-to-one)
- Group channels (department channels, project channels, task-linked channels)
- Message threading
- Read receipts and delivery status
- User presence and availability status
- Message search
- Notification of new messages
- Message retention and archival policies
- Chat linked to tasks, documents, or incidents

**What stays outside:**
- Audio/video calling (Conferencing domain, though it is tightly integrated)
- File storage (FileManagement — files shared in chat are stored there with a reference)
- Real-time delivery infrastructure (handled at infrastructure layer via WebSocket service)

**Data ownership:** Message records, channel definitions, membership records, read states.

**Integrations:**
- FileManagement for shared files
- Conferencing to escalate a chat to a call
- TaskManagement and DocumentManagement for context-linked channels
- IAM for identity
- Notification for message alerts to offline users
- Real-time infrastructure layer for delivery

**Deployment note:** The Communication domain runs in the dedicated real-time service component. Its data and service logic are co-located with the WebSocket infrastructure it depends on.

---

### Domain 8: Conferencing

**Responsibility:** Manages audio and video calls, multi-party conferences, scheduled meetings, recordings, and call history.

**What belongs inside:**
- Call initiation and session management
- Conference room management
- Participant management
- Recording lifecycle (initiation, storage reference, access control)
- Call and conference history
- Scheduled meetings and calendar integration
- Screen sharing session management

**What stays outside:**
- The actual media processing (WebRTC media server infrastructure — e.g., Jitsi, Mediasoup — handles this at the infrastructure layer)
- File storage for recordings (FileManagement)
- Chat during a conference (Communication domain)

**Data ownership:** Call records, conference records, participant logs, recording metadata.

**Integrations:**
- Communication domain for escalating chats to calls
- FileManagement for recording files
- Notification for call invitations
- Media infrastructure for signaling and relay

**Critical design note:** For a government internal platform, the media server must be deployed on-premises. This eliminates all third-party conferencing services. The conferencing domain manages the session logic while the infrastructure layer provides WebRTC signaling (via a self-hosted solution such as Jitsi Meet, BigBlueButton, or a custom Mediasoup deployment).

---

### Domain 9: File Management

**Responsibility:** The centralized file storage layer for the platform. Manages file uploads, storage, versioning, metadata, permissions, and lifecycle across all domains.

**What belongs inside:**
- File upload and ingestion
- File storage abstraction (local storage, object storage — S3-compatible, or NAS)
- File versioning and version history
- File metadata (name, type, size, checksum, classification)
- Folder hierarchy and organization
- File permissions (who can view, edit, download, delete)
- File sharing (with link generation for internal sharing)
- File linking (a file can be linked to a task, a document, a message, an incident)
- File search by metadata
- Virus/malware scanning integration point

**What stays outside:**
- Business logic of the domain consuming a file (a document's approval state is DocumentManagement's concern; a task's status is TaskManagement's concern)

**Data ownership:** File metadata records, folder structure, permission assignments, version history. The physical file bytes live in object storage, referenced by metadata records.

**Integrations:**
- Every domain that handles files references this domain
- Virus scanning service at ingestion
- Audit for file access and deletion events
- Authorization for access control decisions

---

### Domain 10: GIS and Spatial Data

**Responsibility:** Manages geospatial data, map layers, hazard zone definitions, incident location data, and the analytical tools that operate on spatial information. This is the analytical map engine of the emergency management function.

**What belongs inside:**
- Map layer management (administrative boundaries, terrain, infrastructure, hazard zones)
- Incident spatial records (where an incident occurred, affected radius, evacuation zones)
- Hazard zone definitions and severity classifications
- Spatial analysis operations (intersection, proximity, affected area calculation)
- Map tile serving
- Custom layer creation and styling
- Spatial data import and export (GeoJSON, Shapefile, KML)
- Coordinate system management

**What stays outside:**
- Incident reports as documents (DocumentManagement)
- Task assignment for incident response (TaskManagement)
- Analytical dashboard composition (EmergencyAnalytics domain)
- User identity and access (consumed from IAM and Authorization)

**Data ownership:** Spatial datasets, layer configurations, hazard zone records. These live in a PostGIS-enabled database, separate from the transactional core.

**Integrations:**
- EmergencyAnalytics for dashboard widgets and spatial analysis results
- DocumentManagement: incidents documented as records can be enriched with spatial references
- Notification: spatial proximity triggers (e.g., "new hazard zone overlaps with inhabited area")
- FileManagement for imported/exported spatial files

**Deployment note:** This domain deploys with its own PostGIS database and optionally a dedicated tile server (e.g., GeoServer or Martin tile server). It exposes a spatial API consumed by the frontend mapping component.

---

### Domain 11: Emergency Analytics

**Responsibility:** Aggregates, processes, and presents analytical views of emergency data, incident trends, resource utilization, and operational metrics. The intelligence layer of the emergency management function.

**What belongs inside:**
- Emergency incident registry
- Incident classification and categorization
- Statistical aggregations (incident counts by type, region, period)
- Trend analysis
- Dashboard and widget definitions
- Report templates and generated reports
- Data collection forms for field analysts
- Emergency data exchange formats (incoming data from external sources)
- Future: AI/ML model integration points and forecast presentation layer

**What stays outside:**
- Raw spatial data (GIS domain)
- Document management for official reports (DocumentManagement)
- The AI/ML models themselves (Forecasting/AI domain, designed as a pluggable extension)

**Data ownership:** Incident registry, analytical aggregates, report archives, data collection records. A read-optimized analytical datastore (separate from the transactional database) may be appropriate here.

**Integrations:**
- GIS domain for spatial enrichment of incident data
- DocumentManagement for formal incident reports
- FileManagement for exported reports
- Notification for threshold-based alerts
- Forecasting/AI domain as a downstream consumer of incident history and upstream source of forecast overlays

---

### Domain 12: Forecasting / AI (Pluggable Extension)

**Responsibility:** A deliberately thin integration domain that acts as the boundary between the core platform and future AI-based analytical capabilities. Designed now, partially implemented in Phase 2, fully realized in Phase 3+.

**What belongs inside:**
- Model execution interface (accepts incident data, returns forecast output)
- Model registry (which models are active, what their inputs/outputs are)
- Forecast result storage and versioning
- Model training data export interface
- Forecast visualization data API (consumed by GIS and EmergencyAnalytics)

**What stays outside:**
- Model training pipelines (these are data science workloads, not platform components — they run separately and publish model artifacts)
- Incident data ownership (EmergencyAnalytics owns it)
- Map rendering (GIS domain)

**Data ownership:** Forecast records, model metadata, input/output logs.

**Critical design note:** This domain exists primarily as an integration contract. The actual ML infrastructure (Python notebooks, training pipelines, model serving) lives outside the platform and publishes results through this domain's API. Designing the contract now — even before the AI capability exists — ensures the platform can ingest forecast data from any model without architectural disruption.

---

### Domain 13: Notification

**Responsibility:** The platform's unified delivery service for alerts, reminders, and system-generated notifications. Decouples notification production (any domain can raise a notification event) from delivery channel management.

**What belongs inside:**
- Notification event ingestion
- Notification routing (which users receive which notifications)
- Delivery channel management (in-app notifications, email, SMS if configured)
- Notification preferences per user
- Notification history and read status
- Notification templates and localization
- Batching and throttling rules

**What stays outside:**
- The business logic that determines when a notification should be sent (that logic lives in each source domain)
- Message delivery infrastructure (SMTP relay, SMS gateway — external integrations)

**Data ownership:** Notification records, delivery status, user preferences.

**Integrations:**
- All domains publish notification events
- UserManagement for user contact channels
- Communication domain for in-app delivery
- External: email relay, SMS gateway

---

### Domain 14: Audit and Logging

**Responsibility:** Maintains a comprehensive, tamper-resistant record of significant platform events for security, compliance, and operational accountability.

**What belongs inside:**
- Audit event ingestion and storage
- Audit event schema definitions
- Query interface for audit records
- Retention policy management
- Tamper detection (append-only storage, hash chaining if required)
- Audit report generation
- Export for external SIEM integration

**What stays outside:**
- Business logic — this domain observes and records, it does not make decisions
- Application logs (those go to the observability infrastructure, not audit storage)

**Data ownership:** Immutable audit event log. This must be write-once, append-only from a logical perspective. The audit database must be logically isolated — application users must not have delete or update permissions on audit records.

**Integrations:**
- All domains publish audit events
- IAM: authentication events
- Authorization: access denial events, sensitive resource access
- DocumentManagement: all document state transitions
- TaskManagement: assignment and completion events
- Administration: configuration changes, user/role management

---

### Domain 15: Administration and Platform Settings

**Responsibility:** Provides the governance layer for platform administrators — managing users, departments, roles, system configuration, and operational visibility.

**What belongs inside:**
- User lifecycle management (create, suspend, reactivate, delete)
- Role and permission management
- Department and position management (triggers OrgManagement updates)
- System configuration (feature flags, policy settings, integration endpoints)
- License and capacity management
- Health dashboard and operational metrics
- Delegated administration (department admins managing their own users within defined bounds)

**What stays outside:**
- The underlying data owned by other domains (administration triggers operations in other domains — it does not own their data)
- Audit records (read-only consumer of Audit domain)

**Data ownership:** Platform configuration records, delegated admin scope definitions.

**Integrations:**
- Orchestrates operations across IAM, OrgManagement, UserManagement, Authorization
- Reads audit records for administrative review
- Observability infrastructure for operational dashboards

## 7. Cross-Cutting Platform Capabilities

These are not domains — they are platform-level concerns that exist across all modules and must be resolved architecturally, not domain-by-domain.

### 7.1 Authentication and Token Propagation

Every request entering the platform — from any client, via any API — must carry a valid authentication token. The API Gateway validates the token against IAM before the request reaches any domain. Each domain receives the authenticated principal as context but does not re-validate the token. Token format is JWT with short expiry and refresh token rotation. All tokens carry: user ID, position IDs, classification clearance level, and session ID for audit correlation.

### 7.2 Authorization Enforcement

The Authorization domain provides a centralized decision point. All domains must call this before executing any sensitive operation. The call is: `can(userId, action, resourceType, resourceId?)`. No domain implements its own authorization logic. This is enforced through code review and architecture review processes.

The Authorization domain must implement:
- **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC):** static role-permission assignments
- **Hierarchical RBAC:** positional authority inherits from subordinate positions
- **Resource-Level Access Control:** a user may read their department's documents but not another department's

### 7.3 Audit Event Emission

Every domain must emit structured audit events for significant operations. The event bus carries these events to the Audit domain asynchronously. Audit events are never dropped — the event bus must guarantee delivery even if the Audit domain is temporarily unavailable (durable queue). Each audit event includes: timestamp, user ID, session ID, action, resource type, resource ID, result (success/fail), and IP address.

### 7.4 Data Classification

Every sensitive record — documents, files, incident reports, messages in sensitive channels — carries a classification label. The platform supports at minimum: **Unrestricted**, **Internal**, **Confidential**, **Secret**. Classification is stored on the resource and evaluated by the Authorization domain. Users must have a clearance level at or above the resource's classification to access it. Classification is immutable once set (can only be upgraded, never downgraded, by an authorized user).

### 7.5 Localization

The platform must support Tajik (Cyrillic and Latin script), Russian, and English as interface languages. Localization is a platform-level concern handled by a shared localization library, not implemented per-domain. All user-facing strings, notification templates, and document templates must be externalized into locale bundles.

### 7.6 Search

Full-text search across documents, tasks, files, and messages requires a dedicated search index (Elasticsearch or OpenSearch). Each domain publishes indexing events when content changes. The Search infrastructure maintains the index. A unified search API allows cross-domain queries with authorization filtering applied before results are returned — a user must never see search results for content they do not have permission to view.

### 7.7 File Attachments

File attachments are a universal capability. The FileManagement domain exposes an upload API and a reference model. Any domain that handles files stores file references (IDs and metadata) in its own domain model, but never copies file bytes. The FileManagement domain is the single source of truth for file contents and permissions.

### 7.8 Observability

Application metrics, distributed traces, and structured logs flow to a centralized observability stack (Prometheus for metrics, structured log aggregation via Loki or similar, Grafana for dashboards). This is infrastructure-level, not domain-level. Every service exposes a health endpoint. Alerting rules cover service availability, error rates, latency thresholds, and storage capacity.

### 7.9 Configuration Management

Application configuration (database URLs, service endpoints, feature flags, integration keys) must never be hardcoded. A configuration management approach — at minimum environment-based configuration, later a secrets vault — provides runtime configuration injection. No credentials appear in source code or version control.

### 7.10 Activity History

Users and administrators need a human-readable activity history for resources — "Document X was approved by Y on date Z, then forwarded to W." This is distinct from the audit log (which is security-oriented and not directly user-visible). Each domain maintains its own activity log for its resources. A shared activity log library standardizes event structure and storage.

## 8. Module Interaction Strategy

The interaction model reflects where tight consistency is required versus where eventual consistency is acceptable.

### 8.1 Synchronous API calls (tight consistency required)

Used when the outcome of the call must succeed or fail atomically with the calling operation.

**Examples:**
- Creating a task requires an authorization check: `can(userId, "assign-task", "user", assigneeId)`. This must be synchronous — the task must not be created if unauthorized.
- A document being submitted for approval must validate that the approval chain positions exist in OrgManagement before the submission succeeds.
- A file upload returns a file ID that is immediately embedded in the calling resource record.

**Implementation:** HTTP/REST internal calls between modules within the monolith. Because modules share a process, these are function calls with HTTP-shaped contracts — no network round trips in the monolith phase. When a module is later extracted to a separate service, the same contract becomes a real HTTP call with no change to the calling code.

### 8.2 Asynchronous events (eventual consistency acceptable)

Used when the outcome does not need to be guaranteed synchronously and the consuming domain can process the event independently.

**Examples:**
- Document approved → emit `document.approved` event → Notification domain sends notifications to recipients → no need to wait for notification delivery before confirming approval
- Task completed → emit `task.completed` event → Audit domain records completion → EmergencyAnalytics updates incident resolution metrics
- User deactivated → emit `user.deactivated` event → Communication domain marks presence as offline, TaskManagement reassigns pending tasks, DocumentManagement suspends routing to that position

**Implementation:** An internal event bus. In Phase 1 (monolith), this is an in-process pub/sub dispatcher (e.g., an application-level event mediator). It shares the database transaction — meaning the event is published only after the originating transaction commits. In Phase 2+, this can be replaced by an outbox pattern backed by a message broker (RabbitMQ or Kafka) with no change to domain code.

### 8.3 Real-time channels

Used for user-facing real-time delivery: new messages, presence updates, task notifications, call invitations.

**Implementation:** The real-time service (Communication domain's runtime) maintains WebSocket connections to connected clients. The main application publishes events to the real-time service via the message bus. The real-time service delivers them to connected clients. This is the only domain that requires persistent bidirectional connections.

### 8.4 Where tight boundaries are non-negotiable

The following cross-domain interactions must never bypass the service API and must never use direct database access across domain boundaries:

- Authorization decisions must always go through the Authorization domain's decision API
- File access must always go through FileManagement's permission layer
- Audit events must always be emitted — they must never be silently dropped in error paths
- Document routing chains must always be resolved through OrgManagement — never hardcoded

### 8.5 Data consistency strategy

The platform does not need global distributed transactions. Instead:

- **Within a domain:** ACID transactions in the domain's database
- **Across domains (synchronous call):** the calling domain owns the transaction; if the cross-domain call fails, the calling domain rolls back its own transaction
- **Across domains (asynchronous event):** outbox pattern ensures events are published reliably; consuming domains are idempotent

## 9. Data Architecture Foundation

### 9.1 Primary database strategy

**Recommendation:** PostgreSQL as the primary relational database for all transactional domains.

PostgreSQL is enterprise-grade, open-source, has excellent JSONB support for flexible schema needs, supports row-level security for database-layer access control, and — critically — supports PostGIS for spatial data. Using one database engine across all domains reduces operational complexity without sacrificing capability.

**Schema separation strategy:**

In the monolith phase, each domain owns a separate PostgreSQL schema within a single database instance. Schema-level isolation enforces that domain A's tables are never directly joined with domain B's tables (enforced via database role grants — each domain's application role only has access to its own schema). This provides:
- Logical isolation with operational simplicity
- A clear migration path: a domain's schema can be extracted to a separate database instance when independent scaling is required
- Database-level audit of cross-schema access attempts

In Phase 2+, high-write domains (Audit, Communication) may be migrated to dedicated database instances without disrupting others.

### 9.2 Spatial data

PostGIS extension on a dedicated PostgreSQL instance for the GIS domain. Spatial indexes on geometry columns. Coordinate reference system standardization (WGS84 / EPSG:4326 as the interchange standard; projected coordinates for analysis). Tile serving via a dedicated map server, not via the application API.

### 9.3 File and object storage

All file bytes are stored in an S3-compatible object storage system deployed on-premises (e.g., MinIO). FileManagement stores metadata and permissions in PostgreSQL; the object storage holds the bytes. Benefits:
- Files are served directly from object storage (via pre-signed URLs for authorized downloads) without streaming through the application servers
- Storage can be scaled independently from the application
- Backups of file storage are separate from database backups

### 9.4 Search and indexing

OpenSearch (open-source Elasticsearch fork) deployed as a separate service. Each domain publishes indexing events for searchable content. A shared indexing service consumes these events and maintains per-domain indices. All search queries are filtered by authorization context before results are returned. The search index is a derived data store — it can be rebuilt from source records at any time. It is never a primary data store.

### 9.5 Analytics and reporting data flow

Operational reports (document status reports, task completion reports, incident summaries) are generated from the transactional database with read replicas to avoid impacting write performance.

For the EmergencyAnalytics domain, a separate read-optimized store (either a PostgreSQL schema with materialized views, or a lightweight columnar store) aggregates incident data for analytical queries. This is populated from the transactional source via a lightweight ETL process — not via direct queries on the transactional tables.

Real-time analytics dashboards (e.g., live incident count on a map) consume the event bus, not the database, to update dashboard state.

### 9.6 Cache layer

Redis as the shared cache layer for:
- Authorization decision caching (short TTL, invalidated on role/permission changes)
- Session storage (for the real-time service)
- Rate limiting counters
- Notification throttling state
- Frequently read but rarely changed configuration data (org structure cache)

## 10. Security Architecture Foundation

### 10.1 Identity model

Every entity in the platform has a verifiable identity:
- **Human users:** authenticated via IAM, issued JWT tokens
- **Service accounts:** machine identities for internal service-to-service calls, authenticated via short-lived tokens or mutual TLS
- **External integrations:** dedicated service accounts with minimum required permissions and no interactive login

The token carries: `sub` (user ID), `positions` (list of position IDs), `clearance` (classification level), `session_id`, `iat`, `exp`. Short expiry (15–30 minutes) with refresh tokens. Refresh tokens are rotated on each use. Sessions can be revoked server-side by invalidating the session ID in the session store.

### 10.2 RBAC foundation

Three-layer authorization model:

**Layer 1 — Role-Permission RBAC:** A user holds one or more roles. Each role grants a set of permissions (capabilities). Permissions are fine-grained: `document:create`, `document:approve:department`, `task:assign:subordinate`, `gis:layer:edit`, etc.

**Layer 2 — Hierarchical authority:** A user's positional authority in the organizational hierarchy extends their effective permissions downward in the chain. A Deputy Chairman inherits the resource access authority of all department heads below them. This inheritance is computed at authorization evaluation time, not stored as explicit role assignments.

**Layer 3 — Resource ownership and classification:** Even with a granted permission, access to a specific resource is further constrained by: (a) the resource's classification level vs. the user's clearance level; (b) the resource's owning department vs. the user's positional scope; (c) explicit sharing grants.

This three-layer model handles the full range of CoESCD's access control requirements without requiring a custom permission system per domain.

### 10.3 SSO readiness

The IAM domain is designed with a pluggable authentication provider interface. Initial deployment uses local username/password authentication. The interface supports future integration with:
- LDAP/Active Directory (likely if CoESCD has an existing IT directory)
- SAML 2.0 (for government SSO federation)
- OIDC (for modern SSO with supporting identity providers)

The platform's token model (JWT issued internally) is decoupled from the authentication method. Even when SSO is used, the platform issues its own internal token after successful SSO assertion verification — this maintains control over the token's claims and lifetime.

### 10.4 Audit boundaries and tamper resistance

The Audit domain's database is protected by:
- Application-layer append-only access (no UPDATE or DELETE operations from application code)
- Database role that only has INSERT and SELECT on audit tables
- Periodic hash chaining of audit records (each record includes a hash of the previous record's content) to detect retroactive tampering
- Audit records replicated to a secondary, separately administered store for independent verification

Sensitive audit events (access to classified documents, administrative role changes, authentication failures) generate real-time alerts in the Administration dashboard.

### 10.5 Least privilege

- Each domain's application process runs under a database user with access only to its own schema
- Service accounts for inter-service communication have permission only to call the specific APIs they need
- Administrative capabilities are explicitly granted — no user has administrative access by default
- Delegated administration is scoped — a department admin can manage users in their department only, not platform-wide settings

### 10.6 Confidentiality segmentation

Resources are classified at creation. Classification is immutable downward (cannot be declassified without explicit elevated authority). Access to classified resources requires both the RBAC permission and the corresponding clearance level. Search results, notification content, and analytics aggregates respect classification — a user does not see record counts or summaries for classifications above their clearance level.

### 10.7 Secure communication

All client-server communication over HTTPS (TLS 1.3). WebSocket connections use WSS. Internal service-to-service communication within the same host or trusted network uses HTTP, but communication between separately deployed services uses HTTPS with server certificate verification. Media (WebRTC) uses DTLS-SRTP. No unencrypted channels for any platform traffic.

### 10.8 Secrets management

No secrets in code or configuration files checked into version control. Secrets are injected at runtime via environment variables in Phase 1, transitioning to a secrets vault (HashiCorp Vault or equivalent) in Phase 2. Database credentials, API keys, and signing keys are rotated on a defined schedule. Key rotation does not require platform downtime.

## 11. Deployment and Runtime Foundation

### 11.1 Runtime topology

**Phase 1 deployment:**

```
[Client Browser / Desktop App]
        │ HTTPS
[Reverse Proxy — Nginx]
        │
[API Gateway / BFF Layer]
   ├── [Monolith Application Server]  ── [PostgreSQL (multi-schema)]
   │                                  ── [Redis Cache]
   │                                  ── [MinIO Object Storage]
   │                                  ── [OpenSearch]
   ├── [Real-time Service]  ── [Redis Session Store]
   └── [GIS / Spatial Service]  ── [PostGIS Database]
                                 ── [Tile Server]

[Media Server — Jitsi / Mediasoup]  (for audio/video conferencing)

[Observability Stack — Prometheus + Grafana + Loki]
```

All components deployed on-premises or in a government-controlled environment. No external dependencies at runtime.

### 11.2 Reverse proxy and API gateway

Nginx as the reverse proxy and TLS terminator. The API Gateway layer (initially a module in the monolith, later a standalone component) handles:
- Authentication token validation (every request)
- Rate limiting
- Request routing to appropriate backend
- Correlation ID injection for request tracing
- Response compression

### 11.3 Real-time infrastructure

A dedicated Node.js service (or Go-based alternative) manages WebSocket connections using Socket.io or native WebSocket. This service:
- Maintains connection state per user session
- Subscribes to the message bus for real-time events
- Delivers events to connected clients
- Manages presence state in Redis
- Horizontally scalable via Redis-backed pub/sub for multi-instance coordination

### 11.4 Media infrastructure

Self-hosted WebRTC media server for audio/video conferencing. Jitsi Meet is the recommended option — it is open-source, battle-tested, can be fully self-hosted, and provides a complete conferencing solution. Mediasoup is an alternative for a custom implementation with greater control. The media server:
- Handles TURN/STUN for NAT traversal within the government network
- Manages SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit) for multi-party calls
- Interfaces with the Conferencing domain for session management

### 11.5 Storage components

| Component | Purpose | Technology |
|---|---|---|
| Primary DB | Transactional data for all domains | PostgreSQL 15+ |
| Spatial DB | GIS and spatial analysis | PostgreSQL + PostGIS |
| Cache | Sessions, auth decisions, rate limiting | Redis 7+ |
| Object Storage | File bytes | MinIO (S3-compatible) |
| Search Index | Full-text and metadata search | OpenSearch |
| Analytics Store | Aggregated incident analytics | PostgreSQL materialized views (Phase 1), dedicated OLAP (Phase 3) |

### 11.6 Monitoring and observability

- **Metrics:** Prometheus with exporters for PostgreSQL, Redis, Nginx, and application metrics. Grafana dashboards for operational visibility.
- **Logs:** Structured JSON logging from all services. Loki for log aggregation. Logs include correlation IDs for request tracing.
- **Alerts:** On-call notification for service down, error rate spike, storage capacity threshold, failed authentication surge.
- **Health checks:** Every service exposes `/health` (liveness) and `/ready` (readiness) endpoints.

### 11.7 Scaling approach

Phase 1 is single-node with read replicas. Scaling in later phases:
- Application server: horizontal scaling behind load balancer, stateless by design (all state in DB or cache)
- Real-time service: horizontal scaling with Redis-backed session sharing
- Object storage: MinIO cluster mode for distributed storage
- Database: read replicas for heavy read workloads; connection pooling via PgBouncer

## 12. Scalability and Reliability Considerations

### 12.1 High availability considerations

The platform is mission-critical — it must remain available during emergencies. Priority reliability measures:

- **Database:** Primary with hot standby replica using PostgreSQL streaming replication. Automatic failover via Patroni or equivalent.
- **Application:** Deploy at minimum two application server instances behind a load balancer.
- **File storage:** MinIO in distributed mode with erasure coding for data durability.
- **Real-time service:** Multiple instances behind a load balancer with shared Redis state.
- **UPS and power:** Deployment facility must have uninterruptible power supply.

### 12.2 Degraded mode operation

During network disruption or partial system failure, the platform should degrade gracefully:
- Cached authentication tokens remain valid for their lifetime even if IAM is temporarily unavailable
- Document reads should succeed even if approval workflows are temporarily suspended
- Real-time communication should queue messages and deliver on reconnection

### 12.3 Load characteristics

This is not a consumer platform. At CoESCD scale:
- Hundreds to low thousands of concurrent users
- Peak load during major emergency events
- GIS data queries and large file transfers are the most resource-intensive operations
- Communication domain has the highest message throughput but each message is small

The architecture is not premature-optimized for scale. It is designed to handle realistic government institutional load while being extensible for larger load if the platform is adopted by regional or local emergency services.

## 13. Key Architectural Risks and Trade-offs

### Risk 1: Domain boundary violations under schedule pressure

**Risk:** As development accelerates, teams take shortcuts — direct database access across domain boundaries, shared tables, bypassing the authorization API. This accumulates architectural debt that makes later extraction impossible.

**Mitigation:** Architecture review as a mandatory gate for any cross-domain interaction. Automated tests that verify no cross-schema database queries. Code review checklist that includes domain boundary checks.

### Risk 2: The real-time service becomes a data silo

**Risk:** The real-time service maintains its own state that drifts from the main application's model of the same data (e.g., presence state, unread message counts).

**Mitigation:** The real-time service is the delivery layer only — it does not own business data. All business records (messages, call logs) are written to the main database through the Communication domain's API. The real-time service only holds ephemeral connection and session state.

### Risk 3: Authorization complexity breeds inconsistency

**Risk:** The three-layer authorization model (RBAC + hierarchical authority + classification) is complex to implement correctly. Inconsistencies between how different domains interpret the authorization model create security gaps.

**Mitigation:** The Authorization domain is the single decision point. No domain implements its own authorization logic. Authorization rules are tested as explicit policy tests, not just as integration tests of domain behavior.

### Risk 4: GIS data pipeline brittleness

**Risk:** The GIS domain ingests data from external sources (topographic data, administrative boundary updates, field-reported incident data). External data quality is unpredictable. Poorly formed spatial data can corrupt the analytical layer.

**Mitigation:** A data validation and quarantine layer at the GIS ingestion point. All incoming spatial data is validated and staged before being promoted to the live layer. Ingestion errors are surfaced to administrators, not silently swallowed.

### Risk 5: Premature extraction to microservices

**Risk:** Organizational or stakeholder pressure to "modernize" leads to premature extraction of domains into separate services before the team has the operational maturity to manage them.

**Mitigation:** The extraction decision must be driven by demonstrable need — a specific domain must prove it requires independent scaling or deployment cadence before extraction. Each extraction is treated as a significant project, not a refactor.

### Risk 6: Media server sovereignty and performance

**Risk:** A self-hosted WebRTC media server within a government network may have quality issues (latency, packet loss) that degrade the conferencing experience and undermine user adoption.

**Mitigation:** Network quality assessment before media server deployment. TURN server deployed close to users on the LAN. Start with Jitsi as a proven solution; invest in tuning based on real usage data before considering a custom media stack.

### Trade-off: Modular monolith vs. immediate microservices

The modular monolith delivers higher feature velocity, lower operational complexity, and lower risk for the first 18–24 months. The trade-off is that extracting domains later requires disciplined refactoring effort — but this is exactly what good module boundaries make feasible. The alternative (premature microservices) front-loads operational complexity with no feature benefit.

### Trade-off: Single PostgreSQL instance vs. per-domain databases

Single instance with schema separation is simpler operationally and adequate for Phase 1. The trade-off is that a single database failure affects all domains simultaneously. In Phase 2, the Audit domain and the GIS domain should be the first candidates for dedicated database instances, both for performance isolation and security boundary reasons.

## 14. Recommended Evolution Path

### Phase 0: Architecture and Infrastructure Foundation (Pre-development)

This phase is non-negotiable and precedes any feature development.

- Finalize domain model and module boundaries with the development team
- Establish the internal API contract format (request/response shapes for cross-domain calls)
- Establish the audit event schema (every domain will use it)
- Set up the infrastructure: PostgreSQL (multi-schema), Redis, MinIO, OpenSearch, Nginx
- Set up the observability stack: Prometheus, Grafana, structured logging
- Implement the shared cross-cutting library: token parsing, authorization API client, audit event emitter
- Establish the CI/CD pipeline and deployment process

**What gets designed in this phase but not implemented:** AI/ML integration contracts, SSO federation interface, advanced analytics pipeline, media server (scaffolded but not production-ready).

### Phase 1: Identity, Organization, Administration, Document Management, Task Management

The operational core. Without this, users cannot work in the platform.

- IAM with local authentication
- OrgManagement with the full hierarchy model
- UserManagement and position assignments
- Authorization with full three-layer model
- Administration panel (user, role, department management)
- DocumentManagement with routing, approval workflows, and execution control
- TaskManagement with hierarchical assignment and tracking
- FileManagement for document and task attachments
- Notification for document and task events
- Audit domain consuming events from all Phase 1 domains

**End state:** Administrators can configure the organization. Users can log in, manage documents through the full approval lifecycle, assign and track tasks, and attach files.

### Phase 2: Communication, Conferencing, GIS Foundation, Emergency Analytics

The collaboration and operational intelligence layer.

- Communication domain with chat, channels, and task-linked conversations
- Real-time service deployed as a dedicated component
- Conferencing domain with Jitsi integration for audio/video calls
- GIS domain with map layer management, hazard zones, and incident spatial records
- EmergencyAnalytics domain with incident registry and basic reporting
- Search service integration across documents, tasks, and messages
- Enhanced notification: SMS gateway if required

**End state:** The platform supports real-time collaboration, geographic incident awareness, and basic analytics dashboards.

### Phase 3: Advanced Analytics, AI Forecasting Integration, SSO, Enhanced Administration

The intelligence and governance layer.

- EmergencyAnalytics: advanced trend analysis, statistical reporting, historical comparisons
- Forecasting/AI domain: integration contracts with ML models, forecast visualization overlay on GIS
- SSO integration: LDAP or SAML federation if government IT directory exists
- Advanced audit features: hash chaining, SIEM export
- Delegated administration: department-level admin workflows
- Mobile client: read-focused mobile interface for field operators
- Performance optimization based on real production usage data

**End state:** The platform delivers predictive analytics, federates with government identity infrastructure, and supports mobile field operations.

### What remains pluggable

- AI model implementations (the domain contract is fixed; models can be swapped)
- SSO provider (the IAM interface is fixed; the provider is configurable)
- Notification delivery channels (new channels added without domain changes)
- Map data sources (GIS ingestion layer is extensible)
- Media server implementation (Conferencing domain is decoupled from media server choice)

### What must be designed correctly now and cannot change later

- The authorization model (three-layer RBAC + hierarchy + classification)
- The audit event schema (audit records are append-only; the schema must be stable)
- The domain ownership boundaries (which domain owns which data)
- The cross-domain API contract style (synchronous REST vs. asynchronous events)
- The token format and claims structure
- The data classification model (levels and their semantics)

## 15. Final Architecture Recommendation

The CoESCD Unified Digital Platform must be built as a **domain-partitioned modular monolith with two extracted real-time components**, governed by strict inter-domain boundaries, a centralized authorization model, and a tamper-resistant audit fabric.

**The core architectural commitments:**

1. **One authorization model, one decision point.** The Authorization domain is the single source of truth for all access control decisions. No exceptions. This is not only a security requirement — it is what makes the complex hierarchical government access model manageable and auditable.

2. **Positions, not users, own workflows.** Documents route to positions. Tasks are assigned to positions. Approval chains are defined over positions. This reflects how government institutions actually operate and ensures the platform survives personnel rotation without workflow disruption.

3. **Domain ownership is absolute.** No domain queries another domain's database. All cross-domain interaction goes through service APIs. This discipline is what makes the platform maintainable over a multi-year lifetime and what makes future domain extraction a refactoring project rather than a rewrite.

4. **The audit fabric is non-negotiable.** Every significant state change — in every domain — emits a structured audit event. These events are stored immutably. This is both a legal obligation and a security requirement that cannot be retrofitted after the fact.

5. **The modular monolith earns the right to decompose.** Domain extraction to independent services happens when a specific domain proves it needs independent deployment cadence or scaling — not on a theoretical future-proofing basis. The architecture enables this extraction; it does not mandate it prematurely.

6. **Sovereignty first.** Every architectural decision is evaluated against the requirement that the platform can run entirely within government-controlled infrastructure with no external dependencies. Third-party SaaS integrations are a Phase N+ consideration, never a Phase 1 dependency.

This architecture gives CoESCD a platform that starts with manageable operational complexity, delivers meaningful business value in each phase, maintains strict security and governance properties from day one, and creates a clear evolutionary path toward a fully capable digital emergency management institution — without architectural dead ends.
