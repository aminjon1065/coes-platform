# CoESCD Platform — Chat, Audio/Video, and Real-time Architecture

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Real-time communication is the most architecturally demanding component of the CoESCD platform. It combines three distinct engineering challenges — persistent connection management, media transport and processing, and distributed state synchronization — under a single set of government-grade security and auditability requirements. Unlike the transactional domains of the platform, the communication infrastructure operates at millisecond timescales, maintains long-lived server state, and must function reliably precisely when the organization is under stress: during active emergency events when communication load peaks and network conditions degrade.

The recommended architecture separates the communication stack into three distinct layers with independent scaling and deployment characteristics:

**Layer 1 — The Real-time Gateway:** A horizontally scalable WebSocket server cluster that manages persistent client connections, distributes events to connected clients, and handles presence state. This layer is stateless at the application level (all shared state lives in a distributed cache), enabling linear horizontal scaling.

**Layer 2 — The Chat Domain Service:** A stateful domain service that owns message records, channel configurations, message delivery accounting, and the business logic of the chat system. This layer is separated from the connection layer specifically to allow independent scaling and to prevent business logic from becoming entangled with connection management.

**Layer 3 — The Media Subsystem:** A dedicated Selective Forwarding Unit (SFU) cluster for audio/video transport, a TURN/STUN infrastructure for NAT traversal, and a recording pipeline. This layer has entirely different resource characteristics than the other two — it is CPU and bandwidth intensive, not I/O bound — and must be independently scaled and operated.

The architecture is designed for sovereign government deployment: all three layers run entirely on-premises within CoESCD's infrastructure. There are no dependencies on external media relays, external signaling services, or third-party communication platforms. Every byte of operational communication stays within the government network perimeter.

## 2. Role of Communication Module

### 2.1 What the Communication Module Is

The Communication module is the real-time operational nervous system of the CoESCD platform. Where the EDMS handles formal documentary processes and Task Management handles execution accountability, the Communication module handles the live operational coordination that happens between formal actions: the discussion that precedes a decision, the quick verification between a coordinator and a field analyst, the emergency conference call that assembles leadership within minutes of an incident report.

In an emergency management institution, communication speed and reliability are not quality-of-life features — they are operational requirements. A platform that forces officials to switch to WhatsApp or telephone calls for real-time coordination has failed its mission regardless of how well-designed its document workflows are.

### 2.2 What Makes This Different from a Generic Chat System

Three properties elevate this beyond a standard enterprise messaging system:

**RBAC-integrated access control.** Every channel, every direct message capability, and every call initiation is subject to the platform's authorization model. A user cannot join a classified channel without the required clearance level. A junior analyst cannot initiate a call with the Chairman without the platform's authority model permitting that interaction. Channel membership is derived from organizational position, not user choice.

**Full auditability.** Government communication is subject to institutional accountability. The existence of every communication event — who communicated with whom, when, in what channel, for how long — is an auditable institutional record. Message content for sensitive channels is subject to retention policies. This does not mean surveillance — it means the organization can answer accountability questions after the fact.

**Emergency-mode operation.** The communication system must be the last thing that fails during a crisis. Emergency broadcasts, mass notifications to specific positions, and conference calls must work when normal workflows are overwhelmed. The architecture explicitly designs for emergency-mode degradation.

## 3. Domain Boundaries

### 3.1 What the Communication Domain Owns

**Channel records.** The definition of every communication channel: its type (direct, group, department, task-linked, document-linked, emergency), its membership, its classification level, its creation authority, its retention policy, and its configuration.

**Message records.** Every message sent through the platform, with its complete metadata: sender position, channel, timestamp, sequence number, delivery status per recipient, read status per recipient, edit history, and deletion record. Message content is stored in the Communication domain's database (with encryption at rest).

**Delivery and read accounting.** The message delivery and read state tracking: which recipients have received which messages, which have read which messages. This state is maintained by the Communication domain, not by the Real-time Gateway (which handles delivery but does not own the accounting).

**Call session records.** Every call and conference session: participants, start time, end time, call type, recording reference (if any), and the calling authority context (which position initiated the call).

**Presence state.** The current online/offline/away status of every connected user, maintained in the distributed cache and owned at the service level by the Communication domain.

**Channel membership management.** The rules governing who may join which channels, how membership is derived from organizational position, and explicit membership grants for cross-organizational channels.

**Retention and archival policy execution.** The Communication domain applies configured retention policies to message records and enforces archival transitions.

### 3.2 What the Real-time Gateway Owns

The Real-time Gateway is infrastructure, not a domain. It owns:

**Active WebSocket connections.** The mapping of connected clients to their server instances. This state lives in Redis, not in the application process.

**Event routing tables.** The mapping of channel IDs and user IDs to which server instances currently have relevant connections. Used for efficient event fan-out.

**Ephemeral typing indicators.** Typing state is too high-frequency and too low-value to persist. It lives only in the connection layer with a short TTL.

The Real-time Gateway does not own message content, channel definitions, user profiles, or any business data. It is a transport layer.

### 3.3 What the Media Subsystem Owns

**Call session signaling.** The WebRTC signaling exchange (SDP offer/answer, ICE candidates) during call setup.

**Media routing state.** The SFU's internal routing tables for active call sessions.

**Recording streams.** Active recording sessions and their output destinations.

The Media Subsystem does not own call session metadata (that is the Communication domain's record). It owns only the active transport state.

### 3.4 What Stays Outside the Communication Domain

**File bytes.** Files shared in chat are stored in FileManagement. The Communication domain stores the file reference (ID, name, classification).

**Notification delivery.** The Communication domain publishes notification events for offline users. The Notification domain handles delivery.

**Authorization decisions.** The Authorization domain `can()` API is called before channel joins, message sends in restricted channels, and call initiations.

**Audit event storage.** The Audit domain stores communication audit events. The Communication domain emits them.

**User identity.** IAM owns credentials. The Communication domain reads user profiles from UserManagement.

**Org structure.** Automatic channel membership derived from department membership is computed from OrgManagement data, not stored in the Communication domain.

## 4. Chat Architecture

### 4.1 Channel Model

The channel is the fundamental unit of communication organization. Every message belongs to a channel. There are no free-floating messages.

**Channel types and their behavioral implications:**

**Direct Message Channel:** A private channel between exactly two users. Created implicitly when either participant initiates a direct message. Membership is immutable — only the two original participants. Classification level is determined by the higher clearance level of the two participants: a DM between a Secret-cleared official and a Confidential-cleared analyst is classified at Confidential (the lower of the two, because the lower-cleared party is a participant). Direct messages are never accessible to supervisors in operational view — the communication is private by design. Audit metadata (who communicated with whom, when, how many messages) is retained for security audit purposes; content is not exposed through normal supervisory access.

**Group Channel:** A named channel with explicit membership. Created by an authorized user (department head level and above, or users with `channel.create.group` capability). Members are explicitly managed. Classification level is set at creation. Used for project teams, cross-functional working groups, and ad-hoc coordination groups.

**Department Channel:** A channel automatically created for each department in OrgManagement. Membership is derived from organizational position — joining the department's positions automatically includes the user in the department channel. Department channels cannot be deleted; they are created and deactivated with the department itself. Classification is set at the department level.

**Task-Linked Channel:** Automatically created when a task is created (for task types configured to support discussion). Membership includes: the assigning authority, all executors (primary and co-), and any observers. Access derives from task access — if a user can see the task, they can see its discussion channel. The channel lifecycle is linked to the task: when the task is closed, the channel becomes read-only.

**Document-Linked Channel:** Similar to Task-Linked, but tied to an EDMS document. Membership includes all workflow participants. Used for operational discussion during document processing. The channel becomes read-only when the document is archived.

**Emergency Broadcast Channel:** A one-way broadcast channel used for emergency notifications and alerts to defined recipient groups. Emergency channels are write-restricted (only designated emergency communication officials can publish) but read-accessible to all members. Emergency channels are always created with the highest classification level and the broadest appropriate membership.

**System Notification Channel:** A read-only channel per user that receives platform-generated notifications: task assignments, document routing events, deadline alerts. These are system messages, not human communication.

### 4.2 Message Model

A message is a first-class domain record with the following conceptual properties:

**Identity:** A globally unique message ID (generated by the server, not the client) and a channel-scoped sequence number. The channel-scoped sequence number is monotonically increasing within the channel and provides total ordering of messages within that channel.

**Authorship:** The sender's user ID and the position they were occupying at send time. Capturing the position-at-send-time is important for audit and organizational context: a message sent while occupying an acting Deputy Chairman role carries that positional context in the record.

**Content:** The message text (with a configured maximum length), structured attachments (file references), and optional structured content (system messages, notification cards, action prompts).

**Threading:** Optional reference to a parent message ID for threaded replies. Threading is shallow — a reply to a reply is still a reply to the original parent, not a nested tree. This constraint is intentional: deep threading creates navigational complexity that reduces usability in operational contexts.

**Delivery state:** A delivery accounting record per channel member: not_delivered, delivered, read. Delivery means the WebSocket gateway delivered the message to the recipient's connection. Read means the recipient's client sent a read receipt.

**Edit and deletion record:** If a message is edited, the original content is preserved in an edit history record and the message carries an "edited" flag with the edit timestamp. Deletion is soft — the message record persists (for audit) with a "deleted" flag and the content replaced with a deletion marker. Only the sender can delete or edit their own messages, within a configurable time window. Messages in channels with active legal holds cannot be deleted.

**Classification:** A message in a channel inherits the channel's classification by default. A sender with higher clearance may send a message at a higher classification than the channel's default — but this elevates that message's access requirements within the channel.

### 4.3 Message Storage Strategy

Messages are stored in the Communication domain's PostgreSQL database with two access patterns driving the schema design:

**Recent message access** (the last N messages in a channel for active users): Optimized for time-ordered retrieval within a channel. An index on (channel_id, sequence_number) covers this access pattern efficiently.

**Historical search access** (full-text search across message history): Messages are indexed in the platform's OpenSearch index for full-text retrieval. The index is populated asynchronously from message creation events. Classification-aware access filtering is applied at query time.

For the CoESCD scale (hundreds to low thousands of users), a single PostgreSQL database with appropriate partitioning is sufficient. Message records are partitioned by channel_id, which distributes write load across the most active channels and enables efficient per-channel archival and retention enforcement.

Message content encryption at rest is applied at the database level using PostgreSQL's native encryption capabilities. The encryption key is managed through the platform's secrets management infrastructure.

### 4.4 Message Delivery Model

Message delivery follows a two-phase model:

**Phase 1 — Persistence (synchronous):** When a client sends a message, the Chat Domain Service validates the sender's authorization, persists the message record, assigns the sequence number, and returns a message acknowledgement (the assigned message ID and sequence number) to the sender. This is synchronous — the sender knows the message was accepted before Phase 2 begins.

**Phase 2 — Fan-out (asynchronous):** After persistence, the Chat Domain Service publishes a `message.created` event to the platform event bus. The Real-time Gateway subscribes to this event and fans it out to all currently connected members of the channel. Each delivery to a connected client updates the delivery accounting record.

This two-phase model provides at-least-once delivery guarantees and clear acknowledgement semantics: the sender's message was accepted by the server (Phase 1 confirmation), and the server will make best-effort delivery to recipients with retry on reconnection (Phase 2). The sender does not need to wait for all recipients to receive the message — they receive confirmation when the server accepted it.

### 4.5 Message Ordering and Idempotency

Server-assigned, per-channel monotonically increasing sequence numbers provide total ordering of messages within a channel. Clients use the sequence number to detect gaps (missed messages) and to order messages correctly after reconnection.

Client-generated idempotency keys prevent duplicate message submission: if a client sends a message and does not receive an acknowledgement (due to a network interruption), it retries with the same idempotency key. The server stores idempotency keys for a configurable window and returns the original acknowledgement for duplicate submissions without re-persisting the message.

### 4.6 Read and Delivery Status

Read and delivery status is tracked per-user, per-message via a lightweight delivery accounting table. For channels with large memberships, per-message-per-user tracking would be expensive at scale. The optimization: track the **last-read sequence number** per user per channel rather than individual message read states. "All messages with sequence ≤ X have been read by User Y in Channel Z." This reduces the accounting to one record per user per channel, updated on each read receipt.

For direct messages where read receipts are explicitly displayed to the sender, individual message delivery state is maintained. For large department channels, only the unread count (messages with sequence > user's last-read-sequence) is maintained, not individual read states.

## 5. Real-time Infrastructure

### 5.1 Transport Protocol Choice

**WebSocket** is the selected transport protocol. The alternatives and why they are rejected:

**Server-Sent Events (SSE):** Unidirectional — server can push to client but the client cannot push through the same connection. Requires a separate HTTP connection for client-to-server events. Adequate for notification delivery but insufficient for bidirectional real-time communication (typing indicators, read receipts, message sending). Rejected for the primary communication transport.

**Long-polling:** HTTP-based fallback that simulates push by keeping requests open. High server resource consumption, poor latency characteristics, complex client implementation for ordering guarantees. Relevant only as a fallback for severely restricted network environments. Maintained as a degraded-mode fallback.

**HTTP/2 Streams:** More efficient than HTTP/1.1 but still adds overhead for the tight event loop of a real-time chat system. WebSocket provides lower framing overhead for the many small messages characteristic of chat and presence.

**QUIC / HTTP/3:** An emerging transport that handles network path migration more gracefully than WebSocket-over-TCP. Relevant for mobile clients on changing networks. Planned for Phase 3 mobile client implementation but not in the initial deployment.

WebSocket provides: bidirectional communication, low framing overhead, binary frame support (efficient for structured event serialization), wide browser and platform support, and TLS encryption via WSS.

### 5.2 Connection Lifecycle

The connection lifecycle has five phases:

**Phase 1 — TLS Handshake:** The client establishes a TLS 1.3 session with the Real-time Gateway through Nginx. Certificate validation occurs. All subsequent communication is encrypted.

**Phase 2 — WebSocket Upgrade:** The client sends an HTTP Upgrade request to switch to the WebSocket protocol, carrying the authentication token in a header (not in the URL — URL parameters appear in logs).

**Phase 3 — Gateway Authentication:** The Real-time Gateway validates the authentication token against IAM (or against the cached token validation result). On success, the gateway maps the connection to the user's identity, positions, and channel memberships. The connection ID is registered in Redis with the user ID and the gateway instance identifier.

**Phase 4 — State Synchronization:** The gateway sends the client its current presence state, the unread message counts for all channels, and any pending delivery notifications (messages sent while the client was offline). This synchronization packet establishes the client's state baseline without requiring the client to poll.

**Phase 5 — Operational State:** The connection is operational. The client sends events (message send, typing indicator, read receipt, presence update) and receives events (new messages, presence changes, call invitations) through the WebSocket frames.

### 5.3 Connection State in Redis

All connection state that must survive a gateway instance restart or be shared across gateway instances is stored in Redis:

- **User → Connection mapping:** user_id → {connection_id, gateway_instance_id, last_heartbeat_timestamp}
- **Channel → Connected users:** channel_id → set of user_ids currently connected with active connections to that channel
- **Presence state:** user_id → {status, last_seen, current_device}
- **Typing indicator state:** channel_id:user_id → {is_typing, expiry_timestamp}

The gateway process itself holds only the active socket handles. No business state lives in the gateway process memory — a gateway restart loses socket connections (clients reconnect) but loses no data.

### 5.4 Horizontal Gateway Scaling

Multiple gateway instances run behind a load balancer. Sticky sessions (connection affinity) are used for the WebSocket connection duration — a client's WebSocket connection is always handled by the same gateway instance for its lifetime, because the socket handle cannot be moved across instances.

For event fan-out across instances: when a message is published and some channel members are connected to different gateway instances, the Chat Domain Service publishes the fan-out event to a Redis pub/sub channel. Every gateway instance subscribes to the relevant channel pub/sub topics and delivers the event to its locally connected clients. This architecture means the fan-out cost scales with the number of gateway instances, not with the number of connected clients — each gateway instance handles delivery to its own connected clients.

The load balancer uses a consistent hash of the user ID for connection routing. This provides reasonable load distribution while keeping a given user's reconnections on the same gateway instance (reducing Redis reads for state restoration).

### 5.5 Handling Offline Users

When a message is sent and some channel members are offline (no active WebSocket connection):

1. The delivery accounting records their delivery status as `pending`
2. A `notification.pending_message` event is published to the Notification domain for those users
3. When those users reconnect, the Phase 4 state synchronization delivers all pending messages

For long-running offline periods, a batch synchronization is performed at reconnection: the client sends its last-received sequence number per channel, and the gateway responds with all messages since that sequence number. This prevents missed messages regardless of how long the client was offline, up to the message retention limit.

## 6. Event-driven Architecture

### 6.1 Event Bus Design

The Communication module uses the platform's shared event bus (RabbitMQ in Phase 1, promotable to Kafka in Phase 2 as message volume grows). Two event bus usage patterns:

**Internal Communication events** (high frequency, low payload): `message.created`, `message.delivered`, `message.read`, `user.typing`, `presence.changed`, `call.started`, `call.ended`. These are published by the Chat Domain Service and consumed by the Real-time Gateway for delivery to connected clients.

**Platform-level Communication events** (lower frequency, broader consumption): `channel.created`, `channel.member_joined`, `task.discussion_created`, `call.recording_ready`. These are consumed by other platform domains (Task Management for task-linked channel creation, Analytics for metrics, Audit for audit events).

### 6.2 Event Types and Schemas

All events use a shared envelope schema:

```
Event {
  event_id: UUID,
  event_type: string,
  timestamp: ISO-8601 UTC,
  source_service: string,
  correlation_id: UUID (links related events),
  payload: type-specific object
}
```

Key event types and their consumers:

| Event Type | Producer | Primary Consumers |
|---|---|---|
| `message.created` | Chat Service | Real-time Gateway, Audit, Search Indexer, Notification |
| `message.delivered` | Real-time Gateway | Chat Service (delivery accounting) |
| `message.read` | Chat Service | Real-time Gateway (broadcast to sender) |
| `typing.started` / `typing.stopped` | Real-time Gateway | Real-time Gateway (peer distribution only) |
| `presence.changed` | Real-time Gateway | Chat Service, Real-time Gateway (broadcast) |
| `call.initiated` | Communication Service | Real-time Gateway (call invitation delivery) |
| `call.participant_joined` | Media Subsystem | Communication Service, Real-time Gateway |
| `call.ended` | Communication Service | Audit, Recording Service, Analytics |
| `channel.created` | Chat Service | Notification, Audit, Search Indexer |
| `notification.pending_message` | Chat Service | Notification Domain |

### 6.3 Consistency Strategy

**Strong consistency (synchronous, within domain):**
Message persistence, sequence number assignment, and delivery accounting updates are strongly consistent within the Chat Domain Service's database. A sender receives a message ID and sequence number only after the message is durably persisted.

**Eventual consistency (asynchronous, cross-domain):**
Fan-out delivery to connected clients, Search index updates, Analytics ingestion, and Audit event recording are eventually consistent. A brief delay between message persistence and appearance in search results is acceptable. Delivery to connected clients is as fast as the pub/sub fan-out allows (typically sub-100ms on a LAN).

**Ephemeral state (best-effort, no persistence):**
Typing indicators and presence updates are best-effort. A missed typing indicator or a stale presence state is an acceptable degradation — these are low-value signals. They use TTL-based expiry in Redis and are never persisted to the database.

### 6.4 Message Broker Sizing for Phase 1

At CoESCD scale (hundreds to low thousands of users), RabbitMQ in a high-availability cluster (primary + mirror configuration) is appropriate. Message throughput for the Communication module at this scale is well within RabbitMQ's comfortable operating range.

The topology is designed so that when Kafka is adopted (Phase 2 or later), the event schema and consumer contracts require no changes — only the broker configuration changes. Topic partitioning in Kafka will be by channel_id, providing message ordering guarantees within a channel.

## 7. Presence System

### 7.1 Presence States

User presence has four states:

**Online:** The user has an active, authenticated WebSocket connection. Set when a connection completes Phase 3 authentication.

**Away:** The user has an active connection but has been inactive (no events sent) for longer than the configured inactivity threshold (default: 5 minutes). Set automatically by the gateway. The user's connection remains open; they receive events.

**Do Not Disturb:** Manually set by the user. Suppresses non-critical notifications. The user is technically online but has signaled unavailability.

**Offline:** No active WebSocket connection. Set when the last connection for a user closes and is not re-established within the grace period (default: 30 seconds — accounts for page refresh or brief network interruption).

### 7.2 Heartbeat Strategy

The gateway sends a WebSocket ping frame every 30 seconds. The client must respond with a pong within 10 seconds. If no pong is received, the connection is considered dead and closed. The client is responsible for initiating reconnection.

The heartbeat interval and the presence state TTL in Redis are coordinated: the Redis entry for an active connection has a TTL of 2× the heartbeat interval. Each heartbeat refreshes the TTL. If a gateway instance crashes without properly closing connections, the Redis TTL expiry will mark those users as offline within 60 seconds — eventual consistency on presence state in failure scenarios.

### 7.3 Multi-Device Handling

A user may be connected from multiple devices simultaneously (desktop workstation and a laptop during a meeting). Presence rules for multi-device:

- **Online state:** the user is Online if any of their connections is Online
- **Away state:** the user is Away only if all their connections are Away
- **Offline:** the user is Offline only when all connections are closed

The Redis presence record stores a list of connection IDs per user. Each connection has its own state and TTL. The effective user presence is computed as the maximum of all connection states.

Presence updates to channel members are debounced: if a user opens a second connection, the transition to "Online" from "Away" (because the first connection was Away) triggers a single presence update event, not two (one for the new connection and one for the state recomputation).

### 7.4 Presence Visibility and Privacy

Not all users see each other's presence. Presence visibility follows channel membership and organizational hierarchy:

- Direct message contacts: see each other's presence
- Shared channel members: see each other's presence within that channel
- Organizational supervisors: see the presence of positions in their command chain (for operational visibility)
- General users: do not see presence of users outside their channels and command chain

Presence information is not broadcast globally — it is delivered on demand (when a user's channel view opens) and as updates (when a user's presence changes, only relevant observers are notified). This scoping reduces presence broadcast volume significantly compared to naive global broadcast.

## 8. Audio/Video Architecture

### 8.1 Architecture Pattern Choice

Three WebRTC topology patterns exist for multi-party calls:

**Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Mesh:**
Every participant sends their media stream directly to every other participant. For N participants, each sends N-1 streams and receives N-1 streams. CPU and bandwidth costs scale as O(N²). Suitable for 2-person calls with no server infrastructure, but impractical beyond 3-4 participants. Rejected as the primary architecture for a platform that must support group calls and conferences.

P2P is used exclusively for one-to-one calls within the government LAN where network quality is high and no server mediation is required for performance. In this scenario, the TURN server mediates NAT traversal but does not relay media (STUN-only mode when direct P2P is possible).

**Multi-point Control Unit (MCU):**
All participants send their media to the MCU server. The MCU decodes all streams, mixes them into a single composite stream, and sends one stream back to each participant. CPU cost is extreme (decode + mix + re-encode for every participant). Bandwidth to the server scales with participants but the return path to each client is fixed (one mixed stream). The MCU produces a traditional conference room experience (a single mixed view). Rejected due to prohibitive CPU cost and the operational complexity of managing encoder farms.

**Selective Forwarding Unit (SFU):**
All participants send their media to the SFU. The SFU forwards individual streams (without decoding or mixing) to appropriate recipients — a selective but unprocessed relay. Each participant sends one stream to the SFU and receives N-1 streams from it. CPU cost on the server is low (routing, not processing). Bandwidth scales with participants but the SFU handles the multi-path distribution rather than each participant's uplink. Simulcast and SVC encoding allow the SFU to selectively forward different quality tiers of each stream based on recipient network capacity.

**SFU is the selected architecture** for all group calls (3+ participants) because it provides the best balance of server CPU efficiency, media quality flexibility, bandwidth optimization, and implementation maturity.

### 8.2 WebRTC Role and Signaling

WebRTC is the media transport protocol, not a communication architecture — it defines how media is encoded, encrypted, and transported between endpoints. The CoESCD platform uses WebRTC for:

- Audio and video capture, encoding, and decoding in the client browser
- DTLS-SRTP encryption of all media streams (mandatory in WebRTC)
- ICE (Interactive Connectivity Establishment) for NAT traversal
- SDP (Session Description Protocol) for capability negotiation

WebRTC itself does not define how signaling (the exchange of SDP offers/answers and ICE candidates) is performed — that is application-specific. The CoESCD platform uses its existing WebSocket infrastructure for signaling, routed through the Communication domain's call signaling handler.

The signaling flow for a call initiation:

1. Caller sends a `call.initiate` event through their WebSocket connection
2. The Communication domain validates the call authority (can this user call this participant set?)
3. The Communication domain creates a call session record and notifies the SFU to prepare a session
4. The Communication domain sends a `call.invitation` event to each invitee through the Real-time Gateway
5. Accepting invitees send `call.accept` events
6. The Communication domain orchestrates the SDP offer/answer exchange between each participant and the SFU through the WebSocket signaling channel
7. ICE candidate exchange flows through the same WebSocket signaling channel
8. Once ICE negotiation completes, media flows directly from clients to the SFU via DTLS-SRTP

### 8.3 STUN/TURN Infrastructure

Within the government LAN, most WebRTC connections can be established using STUN (a server that helps clients discover their public IP address) without requiring media relay. The STUN server is a lightweight service that can run on the same infrastructure as the Real-time Gateway.

TURN (Traversal Using Relays around NAT) is needed when direct P2P or SFU-direct connections are blocked by symmetric NAT or strict firewall rules. For a government internal network, TURN should be needed rarely if network policies are configured to allow direct UDP flows. However, the TURN server must be deployed as a reliability measure — it is the fallback that ensures calls can be established even in restrictive network segments.

TURN server sizing: TURN media relay is bandwidth-intensive. Even at CoESCD scale, a dedicated server with sufficient bandwidth is recommended rather than running TURN on shared infrastructure. TURN traffic is relayed in plaintext on the server (the media is DTLS-encrypted end-to-end; TURN sees ciphertext). TURN servers must be on the government network perimeter, not exposed to the internet.

### 8.4 Codec Selection

Audio: Opus (mandatory in WebRTC implementations, provides excellent quality at variable bitrates from 8kbps to 512kbps, adapts to network conditions).

Video: VP8 or H.264 (both are mandatory in WebRTC). H.264 preferred for hardware acceleration on client devices. VP9 and AV1 as optional higher-efficiency alternatives for stable connections.

The SFU does not transcode between codecs — all participants must use the same codec for a given call session. Codec negotiation through SDP ensures this. The SFU's simulcast support allows multiple quality tiers (full resolution, medium, low) of the same codec to be sent simultaneously, enabling the SFU to route appropriate quality tiers to different participants based on their available bandwidth.

## 9. Media Server Architecture

### 9.1 SFU Selection and Deployment

**Mediasoup** is the recommended SFU implementation for the CoESCD platform. The selection rationale:

- **Open source with no licensing restrictions:** Critical for a government platform with sovereignty requirements. No vendor dependency.
- **Node.js signaling layer with native C++ media processing:** The signaling and session management are in JavaScript (easy to customize and integrate), but the actual media processing (RTP forwarding, RTCP processing) is in native code for performance.
- **Horizontal scalability:** Mediasoup instances run independently. Session distribution across instances is managed by the Communication domain's call session orchestrator.
- **Simulcast and SVC support:** Essential for bandwidth adaptation in variable-quality government networks.
- **Active development and proven in production:** Used by large-scale enterprise deployments.

Alternative: **Janus WebRTC Gateway**. More mature, C-based, proven at scale. Less flexible for custom integration but more extensively battle-tested. If Mediasoup proves operationally complex for the available engineering team, Janus is the fallback.

Not recommended: Jitsi Meet's Videobridge (JVB) as a standalone component is viable, but Jitsi Meet as a whole product would impose its own auth model and UI, conflicting with the platform's integrated approach.

### 9.2 SFU Cluster Architecture

A cluster of Mediasoup instances behind a session-aware load balancer. Session-aware because all participants in a call must connect to the same SFU instance (or the SFU must be configured for cascaded forwarding between instances for very large calls).

For Phase 1 (CoESCD scale), a small cluster of 2-3 SFU instances handles the expected conference load. The call session orchestrator in the Communication domain tracks which SFU instance is handling which call session and routes all participants to the correct instance.

SFU instance sizing guideline: A single Mediasoup instance on a modern 8-core server can handle approximately 100 concurrent call participants in HD video (720p) before CPU becomes the bottleneck. For CoESCD's scale, 2 instances provide adequate capacity with headroom.

### 9.3 Cascaded SFU for Large Conferences

For all-hands emergency conferences with many participants, a cascaded SFU topology is used: multiple SFU instances each handle a subset of participants, and the instances are connected to each other through SFU-to-SFU media forwarding. A participant on SFU-A receives media from participants on SFU-B through the cascade link, not directly.

This topology is transparent to clients — they connect to their assigned SFU instance, which handles the cascade internally. The Communication domain's call orchestrator manages the cascade topology and the cross-instance media routing.

### 9.4 Bandwidth Optimization

**Simulcast:** Video senders transmit multiple quality tiers simultaneously (e.g., 720p, 360p, 180p). The SFU forwards the appropriate tier to each receiver based on the receiver's reported available bandwidth (via RTCP REMB or TWCC feedback). This is the primary quality adaptation mechanism.

**Spatial and Temporal Scalability (SVC):** For codecs that support SVC (VP9, AV1), a single encoded stream contains multiple quality layers. The SFU forwards the appropriate number of layers without requiring the sender to transmit multiple streams.

**Adaptive Bitrate:** The SFU processes RTCP feedback from receivers and signals to senders to adjust their bitrate. This is particularly important for CoESCD's requirement to support calls under poor network conditions.

**Last-N:** In large conferences, participants only receive video from the N most recently active speakers (configurable, typically 6-8). Silent participants' video is not forwarded. Audio mixing cues (volume level reporting) identify active speakers. This dramatically reduces bandwidth consumption in large conferences.

## 10. Recording Architecture

### 10.1 Recording Design Principles

Call recording for a government platform is not a surveillance feature — it is an institutional accountability and continuity feature. Conference recordings allow officials who missed an emergency briefing to review decisions. Training sessions are recorded for reference. Recordings are subject to the same classification and access control rules as the meetings themselves.

Recording is not enabled by default. It is an explicit action by an authorized participant (the conference organizer or an official with `conference.record` capability). Recording initiation is an audit event. Recording access is controlled.

### 10.2 Recording Pipeline

Recording is performed server-side by a dedicated Recording Service, not by client-side screen capture. Server-side recording captures all participant streams as forwarded by the SFU, ensuring:

- Complete recording regardless of which participant is active
- No dependency on any participant's client capabilities
- Centralized access control over the recorded content
- Consistent format and quality

The Recording Service connects to the SFU as a special participant ("recorder endpoint"). The SFU forwards all audio and video streams to the recording endpoint. The Recording Service receives these streams and writes them to a recording pipeline.

**Recording pipeline stages:**

1. **Capture:** RTP streams received from the SFU
2. **Mux:** Individual participant streams muxed into a single container (Matroska/MKV format with separate tracks per participant, or a mixed MP4)
3. **Process:** Optional server-side mixing for a traditional conference-room view (if MCU-style mixed recording is required alongside the SFU architecture). This processing is offline — it happens after the call ends, not in real-time.
4. **Store:** The recording file is stored in FileManagement's object storage (MinIO). The Recording Service calls FileManagement's upload API.
5. **Index:** A recording metadata record is created in the Communication domain: session ID, participants, start/end time, duration, classification, storage reference.

### 10.3 Recording Storage and Access Control

Recordings are stored in FileManagement as classified files. The classification of the recording inherits the classification of the meeting session (set by the organizer at session creation). Access to the recording requires:

- Membership in the original meeting (participants have automatic access)
- A post-meeting explicit access grant (for officials who need to review but did not attend)
- Administrative access (for compliance and audit purposes, requiring elevated authorization)

Recording downloads are audit-logged. Each access to a classified recording produces an audit event.

### 10.4 Retention Policy

Recording retention follows the classification and purpose of the meeting:

- Routine coordination calls: configurable retention (default: 90 days)
- Emergency response conferences: extended retention (minimum 1 year, aligned with incident records)
- Training and instructional recordings: configurable based on content validity

Retention policy enforcement runs as a scheduled process in the Communication domain. Expired recordings are flagged for deletion review, not automatically deleted — a human review step ensures that recordings that may be relevant to ongoing investigations or proceedings are preserved.

## 11. Access Control Model

### 11.1 Chat Access Control

**Direct message access:** Any authenticated platform user may initiate a direct message with any other user they share a channel or organizational relationship with. The authorization check: `can(userId, 'message.direct', 'user', targetUserId)`. Users with no organizational relationship (no shared channel, no command chain connection) cannot initiate direct messages — this prevents unsolicited communication from any user to any other user.

**Channel access:** Channel membership determines message read and write access. Membership sources:
- Automatic (department channels, task-linked channels, document-linked channels): derived from organizational position and workflow participation
- Explicit (group channels): granted by channel admin (the channel creator or department head)
- System grant (by `channel_admin` role): for specific operational needs

Within a channel, read and write access can be differentiated: Emergency Broadcast channels have unrestricted read but restricted write (only authorized broadcasters).

**Message deletion:** The sender may delete their own messages within the configured time window. Channel administrators may delete any message. Administrators may never delete audit records of deleted messages — the deletion event itself is permanent.

### 11.2 Call and Conference Access Control

**One-to-one call initiation:** The caller must have established communication context with the callee — either a shared channel or an organizational command chain relationship. The authorization check prevents cold-calling across organizational boundaries without established context.

**Conference access:** Conference rooms are created by authorized users (`conference.create` capability). Participants must be explicitly invited or invited by position/department. The conference inherits the classification level set by the organizer. Participants must have clearance ≥ the conference classification level to join.

**Conference recording:** Requires `conference.record` capability, available to conference organizers and officials with specific authority grants. Recording initiation is broadcast to all participants as a system message — there are no covert recordings. All participants are informed the moment recording begins.

### 11.3 Classification and Clearance

Channel classification levels use the same five-level scheme as the rest of the platform. A user whose current effective clearance is below a channel's classification level:
- Cannot see the channel in their channel list
- Cannot receive messages from that channel
- Cannot join calls in that channel
- Can see the channel exists if it is a shared task-linked or document-linked channel (where they have contextual awareness) but sees only metadata, not content

## 12. Security Model

### 12.1 Transport Encryption

All client-to-server communication uses TLS 1.3 (WSS for WebSocket connections). TLS termination occurs at the Nginx reverse proxy. Internal communication between platform components (Chat Service to Real-time Gateway, Chat Service to Communication domain) uses TLS for separately deployed components and trusted-network HTTP for co-located components.

WebRTC media is encrypted with DTLS-SRTP (Datagram Transport Layer Security - Secure Real-Time Transport Protocol) — mandatory in all WebRTC implementations. DTLS handshake occurs during ICE negotiation. The DTLS keys are ephemeral per session (perfect forward secrecy). The SFU terminates DTLS from each participant and re-encrypts for forwarding — the SFU sees plaintext media in transit within the government network. This is the architectural constraint of an SFU: the server must be able to forward media, which requires it to be in the media path. For a government internal platform, the SFU is a trusted internal component and this is acceptable.

### 12.2 End-to-End Encryption Trade-offs

True end-to-end encryption (E2EE) for group calls in an SFU architecture is technically possible (using Insertable Streams / WebRTC Encoded Transform) but imposes significant constraints:

**The E2EE benefit:** The SFU cannot read media content even if the server is compromised.

**The E2EE cost:**
- Recording becomes impossible without each participant's decryption key — which requires a key escrow system
- Server-side quality adaptation (simulcast tier selection based on bandwidth) is limited because the server cannot inspect the media
- Administrative compliance access to recorded content requires key management infrastructure
- Implementation complexity is significantly higher

**Recommendation:** E2EE is not implemented in Phase 1. The government network perimeter is the primary security boundary. The SFU is a trusted internal component on a controlled network. DTLS-SRTP provides encryption in transit. At-rest encryption protects recordings. Server-side E2EE (Insertable Streams) is designed as a Phase 3 option, specifically for the highest-classification meetings, with the understanding that it disables recording and reduces some quality adaptation capabilities.

### 12.3 Token and Session Validation

The WebSocket connection uses the platform JWT for authentication. The Real-time Gateway validates the token at connection establishment. Token validity is checked periodically during the connection lifetime (on every significant operation and at configurable intervals) — a token that was valid at connection time but has since been revoked (user suspended, session terminated) will cause the connection to be closed.

SFU connections use short-lived session tokens generated by the Communication domain specifically for the call session. These tokens are generated after the `call.accept` event and are scoped to the specific call session. A media session token cannot be used to join a different call.

### 12.4 Message Content Security

Messages at rest are encrypted using database-level encryption (PostgreSQL pgcrypto or Transparent Data Encryption). Encryption keys are managed through the platform's secrets vault. Key rotation is performed on a defined schedule without requiring message re-encryption (new keys encrypt new messages; old keys decrypt old messages until they age out of retention).

Classification-level messages require logging of access. The audit system records every read of messages in channels classified at Level 3 (Restricted) and above, beyond just the delivery receipt.

## 13. Message and Media Storage

### 13.1 Message Database Architecture

Messages are stored in the Communication domain's PostgreSQL schema, partitioned by channel to enable efficient per-channel queries and per-channel archival. The partition key choice (channel_id) is driven by the primary access pattern: "give me the last N messages in this channel." A time-based partition key would complicate this query across partition boundaries.

**Message record retention tiers:**

- **Active tier** (PostgreSQL): Messages within the configured retention window (default: 1 year for standard channels, 3 years for department channels, 7 years for task-linked and document-linked channels where the discussion is part of the institutional record)
- **Archive tier** (PostgreSQL with cold storage backing): Messages past the active retention window but within the legal hold period
- **Deletion**: After the retention and legal hold period expires, messages are deleted. Deletion is logged in the audit system — the metadata of deleted messages (sender, timestamp, channel, classification, deletion event) is preserved even after content deletion.

### 13.2 Search Index for Messages

Message metadata and content (for channels where content indexing is permitted based on classification) are indexed in OpenSearch. The message index supports:
- Full-text search by content
- Metadata search (sender, channel, date range, classification)
- Phrase matching and proximity search

Content indexing is disabled by default for channels at classification Level 3 and above. The risk of full-text search exposing sensitive content through search snippets outweighs the retrieval convenience. For classified channels, only metadata is indexed.

### 13.3 Media and Recording Storage

Files shared in chat are stored in FileManagement's object storage (MinIO). The Communication domain stores only the file reference.

Call recordings are stored in a dedicated object storage bucket in MinIO, separate from general file storage. The separation enables:
- Different access control policies (recording access is more restricted than general file access)
- Different retention policy enforcement
- Easier compliance auditing (all recordings in one place)
- Potential for different hardware tier (recordings can be large; cold storage tiering saves cost)

Recording files are encrypted at rest using server-side encryption with keys managed by the platform's secrets vault.

## 14. Notification Strategy

### 14.1 In-App Real-time Notifications

For online users (active WebSocket connection), new messages and call invitations are delivered through the real-time connection — no separate notification mechanism required. The Real-time Gateway delivers the event, the client UI updates.

### 14.2 Offline User Notifications

For offline users, the Communication domain publishes `notification.pending_message` events to the Notification domain. The Notification domain handles delivery channel selection (in-app notification queue for next login, email for configured users, SMS for Critical priority messages from Emergency Broadcast channels).

The communication between the Communication domain and the Notification domain is deliberately one-way: the Communication domain publishes events and does not concern itself with delivery. Delivery confirmation from the Notification domain does not feed back into message delivery accounting — the accounting records `delivered` only when the WebSocket delivery to the client occurs.

### 14.3 Call Invitation Handling for Offline Users

If a call invitation targets an offline user, the invitation is delivered through the Notification domain with a ring notification. The offline user's reconnection within the configured invitation window (default: 60 seconds) allows them to join the call. After the window expires, the invitation is marked as missed and delivered as a missed-call notification.

### 14.4 Emergency Broadcast Notifications

Emergency Broadcast Channel messages use the highest notification priority tier. The Notification domain delivers these through all available channels simultaneously (in-app, email, SMS if configured) without user preference filtering. Emergency broadcasts bypass notification batching — they are delivered immediately to every member.

## 15. Platform Integration

### 15.1 Integration with Task Management

When a Task is created (for task types configured with discussion channels), the Communication domain receives a `task.created` event and creates a Task-Linked Channel. The channel is created with:
- Name derived from the task title
- Classification inherited from the task's classification
- Initial membership: assigning authority position, responsible executor position, co-executor positions

The task's chat button in the Task Management UI provides a deep link to this channel. Messages in the channel appear in the Communication domain's chat interface with the task context visible.

When the task transitions to CLOSED state, a `task.closed` event causes the channel to become read-only. Historical messages remain accessible.

### 15.2 Integration with EDMS

Document-Linked Channels are created for documents where operational discussion is configured (document types with `discussion_channel: true` in their template). The channel lifecycle mirrors the document's workflow lifecycle.

The EDMS and Communication domain exchange document reference context: the Communication domain knows the document ID for context display; the EDMS knows the linked channel ID for surfacing the discussion. No message content is stored in the EDMS — the link is a reference only.

### 15.3 Integration with FileManagement

Files shared in chat are processed through FileManagement:
1. Client uploads the file to FileManagement directly (pre-authenticated upload URL)
2. FileManagement returns the file ID and checksum
3. Client sends a message with the file reference to the Chat Service
4. Chat Service validates the file reference (checks it exists in FileManagement and the user is the owner)
5. Chat Service stores the file reference as the message attachment
6. Recipients access the file through a FileManagement pre-signed URL (time-limited, user-specific)

Virus scanning in FileManagement blocks infected files before the message with that reference is accepted.

### 15.4 Integration with the Audit Domain

The Communication domain emits audit events for:
- Channel creation (who, what type, what classification)
- Membership changes (who was added/removed, by whom)
- Every message read in channels at Classification ≥ Level 3
- Call initiation and termination (participants, duration, recording status)
- Recording initiation (by whom)
- Recording access (who accessed which recording)
- Message deletion (who deleted, what channel, timestamp — content not included in audit event)
- Emergency broadcast sends

The Real-time Gateway emits audit events for:
- Connection establishment and authentication (user, timestamp, IP address, device)
- Connection termination
- Authentication failures

## 16. Scalability Strategy

### 16.1 Real-time Gateway Scaling

The Real-time Gateway is designed to be stateless at the process level (all shared state in Redis), enabling horizontal scaling without session affinity constraints beyond the WebSocket connection duration.

Scaling triggers:
- CPU utilization of gateway instances > 70% sustained → add instance
- Active connections per instance > 5,000 → add instance (configurable based on server hardware)
- Redis pub/sub message delivery latency > 50ms → investigate Redis cluster sizing

For CoESCD's initial deployment, 2-3 gateway instances behind the load balancer provide adequate capacity with high availability. A single instance handles several thousand concurrent WebSocket connections on modest server hardware.

### 16.2 Chat Service Scaling

The Chat Domain Service is primarily database-bound (message persistence and retrieval). Horizontal scaling of the service layer is straightforward (stateless service, database is the shared state). Database scaling uses read replicas for heavy read workloads (history retrieval, search) and connection pooling (PgBouncer) to manage the database connection count as service instances scale.

The message fan-out event pipeline (published to the event bus and consumed by the gateway) is the primary throughput bottleneck during peak messaging. The event bus (RabbitMQ / Kafka) is scaled independently of the chat service.

### 16.3 SFU Scaling

SFU instances are CPU-bound (RTP forwarding, RTCP processing). Scaling is driven by concurrent call participants, not concurrent users.

SFU scaling approach:
- Each SFU instance handles a defined number of concurrent call sessions (determined by server hardware testing)
- The Communication domain's call orchestrator distributes new call sessions across SFU instances using a least-loaded selection
- SFU instances can be added to the cluster without disrupting existing sessions
- SFU instances are drained before removal (new sessions not assigned, existing sessions complete)

### 16.4 Database Partitioning and Archival

As message volume grows, the channel-partitioned message table supports efficient per-channel archival: old channel partitions are moved to a cold storage tier (cheaper storage, slower access) while recent partitions remain on fast storage. This tiered storage approach allows the message database to grow indefinitely without proportional storage cost.

## 17. Failure Handling

### 17.1 Message Delivery Reliability

**Client-side reconnection:** Clients implement exponential backoff reconnection with jitter (to prevent thundering herd on server restart). After reconnection, the client sends its last-received sequence number per channel and receives missed messages.

**At-least-once delivery:** The event bus delivers message events with at-least-once guarantees. The gateway's delivery confirmation back to the Chat Service (after successful WebSocket delivery) allows the Chat Service to update delivery state. If a delivery event is lost, the recipient's reconnection synchronization covers the gap.

**Idempotent message processing:** The gateway's message delivery to clients is idempotent — delivering the same message twice (due to at-least-once event bus semantics) is handled by the client's sequence number deduplication. A message with a sequence number the client has already received is silently ignored.

### 17.2 Gateway Instance Failure

When a gateway instance fails:
- Active WebSocket connections on that instance are severed
- Redis connection records for those connections expire within 2× heartbeat interval (60 seconds)
- Clients reconnect to available gateway instances (load balancer detects the failed instance and stops routing to it)
- After reconnection, clients receive missed messages through the sequence-based synchronization

The Redis pub/sub subscription for the failed instance is no longer active, so events destined for clients on that instance are not delivered until those clients reconnect. This is an expected degradation during instance failure — no messages are lost (they are persisted in the database), but delivery may be delayed by the reconnection time.

### 17.3 SFU Instance Failure

SFU failures are more disruptive — active call sessions on the failed instance are terminated. Call reconnection is not automatic (unlike WebSocket reconnection) because re-establishing ICE connections and rejoining a call session requires explicit user action.

Mitigation strategies:
- Redundant SFU instances (active-active with session distribution) minimize the probability of a failure affecting a large fraction of calls
- The Communication domain monitors SFU health through heartbeats; unhealthy instances are drained (no new sessions) before they fail
- Clients receive a clear call termination event with a reconnect-available flag when their SFU instance fails, allowing them to re-initiate the call
- For critical emergency conferences, a "call recovery" procedure allows the organizer to resume the conference on a different SFU instance with participants rejoining

### 17.4 Database Failure

The Communication domain's PostgreSQL database uses a primary-replica architecture with automatic failover (Patroni). During a failover:
- Write operations (new messages, state updates) are unavailable for the duration of the failover (typically 10-30 seconds)
- Read operations continue from the replica
- WebSocket connections remain open; the gateway can still deliver cached events from Redis
- New messages sent during the failover window are queued by clients and retried on reconnection

### 17.5 Degraded Mode Operation

The communication system should degrade gracefully under partial failure, prioritizing in order:

1. **Emergency broadcast delivery:** Highest priority. Even in degraded mode, emergency broadcasts to connected users must be delivered. The gateway maintains a direct Redis-based broadcast path that does not depend on the full Chat Service pipeline.

2. **Active call continuation:** In-progress calls continue unaffected by Chat Service failures — the SFU media path is independent of the Chat Service.

3. **Message delivery to connected users:** Maintained through the Redis pub/sub path even if database writes are temporarily unavailable (messages may be delivered but temporarily not persisted — an acknowledged degradation with recovery on database restoration).

4. **Message persistence and history:** May be temporarily unavailable during database failure. Clients are notified that message history is temporarily unavailable.

## 18. Future Extensions

### 18.1 Broadcast Alert System

The Emergency Broadcast Channel is the Phase 1 foundation for a more capable emergency alert system. Phase 2 extensions:

**Geographic broadcast:** Integration with the GIS domain to send alerts to users whose positions are associated with specific geographic regions or affected areas.

**Role-based broadcast:** Alerting all users in specific positions (all department heads, all emergency analysts) simultaneously through a dedicated emergency channel, bypassing normal channel membership rules.

**Multi-channel alert delivery:** Broadcasting simultaneously through the platform chat, SMS (via the Notification domain), and potentially integration with national emergency alert systems.

**Read confirmation tracking:** Emergency broadcasts require acknowledgement — the system tracks which positions have acknowledged the alert and escalates to unacknowledged positions' supervisors.

### 18.2 Mobile Push Notifications

Phase 3 will include native mobile clients (iOS/Android) for field operators who need read-only access to tasks, documents, and communication from the field. Mobile integration requires:

- APNs (Apple Push Notification Service) and FCM (Firebase Cloud Messaging) integration in the Notification domain for push delivery when the mobile app is in the background
- JWT-based mobile session management with device registration
- Message synchronization protocol for mobile (efficient differential sync, not full history download)
- WebRTC on mobile for call participation (available in modern mobile browsers and native WebRTC SDKs)

The sovereign deployment constraint applies to push notifications: government data in push notification payloads must not be sent through Apple or Google servers. Push notifications must carry only non-sensitive metadata ("You have a new message in [channel name]") and the client fetches actual content from the platform server after waking. This is the standard privacy-preserving push notification pattern used by secure messaging applications.

### 18.3 External Integration Points

Future interoperability with government inter-agency communication systems:

**SIP/PSTN gateway:** Integration with traditional telephone systems through a SIP trunk, allowing calls to and from non-platform users (field personnel without platform access, partner agencies). The gateway translates between WebRTC and SIP, routes calls through the Communication domain's session management, and applies the same access control and audit requirements.

**XMPP federation:** If other government agencies adopt XMPP-based communication platforms, federation with the CoESCD platform allows cross-agency messaging through a controlled gateway. Cross-agency messages are subject to specific classification constraints and are routed through an inter-agency channel with elevated audit requirements.

**Video interoperability (SIP/H.323):** For compatibility with legacy government video conferencing systems (hardware room systems), a transcoding gateway bridges WebRTC to SIP video or H.323. This is a Phase N+ consideration but the architecture supports it through the SFU's existing gateway participant model.

## 19. Final Communication Architecture

The CoESCD real-time communication system is a **three-layer, government-sovereign, authority-integrated communication infrastructure** organized around six architectural commitments that are non-negotiable for a mission-critical government communication system:

---

**Commitment 1 — Sovereign infrastructure, no external dependencies.**
Every byte of operational communication — text messages, voice, video, recording — flows through government-controlled infrastructure. There are no dependencies on external media relays, external push notification services for sensitive content, or third-party communication platforms. The STUN/TURN servers, the SFU cluster, the WebSocket gateway, and the message database are all deployed within the government network perimeter. Sovereignty is not a future goal — it is a Day 1 requirement.

---

**Commitment 2 — Communication access follows organizational authority.**
Channel membership, call initiation rights, and broadcast capabilities are not user-managed free-for-all features. They are governed by the organizational hierarchy, the classification model, and the authorization framework of the platform. A user cannot access a channel they are not authorized for. An analyst cannot cold-call the Chairman. An emergency broadcast cannot be sent by an unauthorized user. The communication system enforces the same authority model as the rest of the platform.

---

**Commitment 3 — The connection layer is separate from the business layer.**
The Real-time Gateway manages socket connections. The Chat Domain Service manages messages and channels. The Media Subsystem manages call transport. These three layers scale independently, fail independently, and are deployed independently. A gateway instance restart causes brief client reconnections but does not lose messages. An SFU failure terminates active calls but does not affect message history. This separation is the architectural property that makes the system operable and maintainable.

---

**Commitment 4 — Every significant communication event is auditable.**
Who communicated with whom, through which channel, at what time, and whether a call was recorded — these are institutional records. The Communication domain emits structured audit events for all security-significant communication actions. The audit trail is permanent. The system does not provide a mechanism to make communication events disappear from the audit record.

---

**Commitment 5 — The system degrades gracefully under stress.**
Emergency events create communication peaks precisely when reliability matters most. The architecture designs for this explicitly: active calls continue during Chat Service failures, emergency broadcasts have a direct Redis-backed delivery path, and client reconnection is automatic and data-safe. The system surfaces degradation to users and administrators rather than failing silently.

---

**Commitment 6 — Extension points are designed, not retrofitted.**
Emergency broadcast escalation, mobile push notifications, SIP gateway integration, and E2EE for classified meetings are not promised features for a distant future release — they are designed extension points in the current architecture. The Emergency Broadcast Channel type exists in Phase 1. The recording architecture's key escrow hook exists in Phase 1. The mobile notification infrastructure's non-sensitive payload constraint is designed in Phase 1. When these capabilities are implemented, they extend the architecture without requiring it to be rebuilt.
