# CoESCD Platform — GIS, Analytics, and AI Forecasting Architecture

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The GIS, Analytics, and AI Forecasting module is the intelligence layer of the CoESCD platform. While the EDMS manages formal documentary processes and Task Management controls operational execution, this module answers the harder questions: Where are the active threats? Which regions face the highest cumulative risk? What does historical incident data predict about next season's flood exposure? How do current field conditions compare to the pattern that preceded the 2015 landslide event?

These are not reporting questions. They are operational questions that directly inform leadership decisions, resource deployment, evacuation orders, and international assistance requests. The architecture must therefore treat this module not as a dashboard subsystem attached to the platform, but as a first-class operational intelligence system with its own data platform, its own processing pipelines, and its own AI/ML inference infrastructure.

Tajikistan's geography imposes specific design constraints that distinguish this system from generic emergency management analytics platforms. The country is approximately 93% mountainous. Seismic activity is constant. Spring snowmelt creates predictable but variable flood windows in the Vakhsh, Panj, and Kofarnihon river basins. Landslide risk correlates with precipitation patterns, slope gradients, and soil saturation across thousands of catalogued unstable slopes. Fire risk spikes in summer across arid southern regions. The analytical and forecasting systems must be built around these specific hazard types, their specific data requirements, and their specific spatial distribution — not around generic emergency management templates.

The recommended architecture is a **three-layer spatial intelligence platform**:

**Layer 1 — The Spatial Data Platform:** PostGIS-backed spatial databases, vector and raster data management, and a tile server for efficient map delivery. This layer serves both the operational GIS visualization needs and feeds the analytical and ML layers.

**Layer 2 — The Analytics Platform:** A combination of PostgreSQL materialized views for operational dashboards and a columnar analytical store (TimescaleDB or ClickHouse) for heavy analytical queries. An ETL pipeline moves data from the operational and spatial layers into the analytical store on a defined schedule.

**Layer 3 — The AI/ML Platform:** A Python-based ML pipeline that consumes historical incident data, spatial risk factors, and environmental monitoring data to train and serve predictive models. ML outputs are returned to the spatial layer as risk score layers visualized on operational maps.

## 2. Role of GIS & Analytics Module

### 2.1 Operational Purpose

The GIS and Analytics module serves three distinct user populations with different needs that must be served by the same underlying data platform:

**Field-level operators and analysts** need real-time map views: where is the current incident, what is the affected zone, what infrastructure is in the hazard area, what evacuation routes are available. Their tool is the interactive operational map with current layers.

**Department heads and analytical staff** need trend analysis and aggregated reporting: how many incidents occurred in this region this quarter compared to last year, which hazard type is increasing, where is response time degrading. Their tool is the analytical dashboard with filtering, export, and trend visualization.

**Senior leadership and the Chairman's office** need strategic intelligence: which regions carry the highest compound risk, what is the forecast for the coming season, how does current preparedness compare to risk exposure. Their tool is the executive intelligence dashboard and the AI-generated risk assessments.

### 2.2 Why This Is Not a Simple Map Viewer

Three properties elevate this beyond a standard GIS viewer attached to a reporting system:

**The data platform is active, not passive.** The system does not just display data entered by users. It ingests external environmental data (meteorological feeds, seismic monitoring, satellite imagery), validates it, integrates it with historical incident data, and makes it available for analysis. The system is a data platform that actively maintains its own knowledge base.

**The analytics are causal, not just descriptive.** Standard BI dashboards describe what happened. This system must support causal analysis: not just "how many floods occurred in Khatlon region," but "what combination of upstream precipitation, soil saturation, and slope conditions precedes flood events in Khatlon region." This requires multi-dataset spatial correlation, not simple aggregation.

**The AI outputs are operational products, not background research.** When the forecasting system produces a risk map for the coming 30-day window, that map is an operational product reviewed by senior analysts, validated against current conditions, and potentially triggers preparedness actions. AI forecasting outputs are integrated into the operational GIS interface as named risk layers, not presented as separate research tools.

## 3. Domain Boundaries

### 3.1 What the GIS Domain Owns

**Spatial datasets and layer catalog.** The registry of all spatial datasets managed by the platform: administrative boundaries, infrastructure, terrain, hazard zones, incident locations, and computed risk layers. Each dataset has a defined owner, classification level, update cadence, and provenance record.

**Geometry storage and spatial indexing.** All geometry data (points, lines, polygons, multigeometries, raster extents) lives in the PostGIS database owned by the GIS domain. No other domain stores geometry independently.

**Layer definitions and symbology.** How layers are visualized: color schemes, classification breaks, symbology rules, scale-dependent rendering configurations.

**Map tile generation and caching.** The tile server infrastructure that renders and caches spatial data as map tiles for client consumption.

**Spatial query processing.** The processing of spatial queries: intersection tests, proximity calculations, area aggregations, containment checks. Spatial queries are executed by the GIS domain's query service, not by other domains directly accessing the PostGIS database.

**Layer access control records.** Which roles, positions, and clearance levels may access which layers. Layer permissions are stored in the GIS domain and enforced by the authorization integration.

### 3.2 What the Analytics Domain Owns

**Incident registry and classification.** The canonical record of emergency incidents: type, location, severity, affected area, response time, resource deployment, resolution. Incidents are the primary analytical unit.

**Aggregated metrics and materialized views.** Pre-computed analytical summaries: incident counts by region/type/period, response time statistics, resource utilization metrics. These live in the analytical store, not in the operational database.

**Report templates and generated reports.** The configuration of report types, their data queries, their output formats, and the archive of generated report files.

**Dashboard definitions.** The configuration of analytical dashboards: which widgets appear, what data they display, who can see them.

**Data collection forms.** The structured forms used by field analysts and operators to report incidents and conditions. Form definitions, validation rules, and submitted responses are Analytics domain data.

### 3.3 What the AI/ML Domain Owns

**Model registry.** The catalog of trained models: model type, version, training date, input features, output schema, performance metrics, and deployment status.

**Feature store.** The curated, versioned set of features used for model training and inference. Features are derived from GIS data, incident history, and external environmental data, and stored in a form optimized for ML consumption.

**Training pipeline state.** The configuration and execution history of model training runs.

**Prediction outputs.** The results of model inference: risk scores, predictions, anomaly flags. These are stored in the AI/ML domain and published to the GIS domain as named risk layers.

**Model performance monitoring records.** Tracked drift metrics, prediction accuracy over time, and model retraining triggers.

### 3.4 What Stays Outside These Domains

**Incident response documents and formal reports** — EDMS domain. The GIS domain holds the spatial record (where the incident occurred, what area was affected). The EDMS holds the official document record of the incident (the registered report, the response order, the after-action assessment).

**Response task assignments** — Task Management domain. When GIS analysis triggers a response requirement, the triggered tasks are created in Task Management. The GIS domain holds the spatial trigger record; Task Management holds the operational execution.

**File bytes for datasets and imagery** — FileManagement domain. Large dataset files, satellite imagery, and exported map files are stored in MinIO via FileManagement. The GIS domain holds the metadata and spatial index; FileManagement holds the bytes.

## 4. Spatial Data Architecture

### 4.1 Primary Spatial Database

**PostgreSQL with PostGIS extension** is the spatial database foundation. The choice is architecturally correct for this platform for several reasons:

PostGIS is the most capable open-source spatial database extension available, providing hundreds of spatial functions implementing the OGC Simple Features standard and beyond. It supports every geometry type needed for emergency management (points for incident locations, lines for rivers and roads, polygons for administrative boundaries and hazard zones, multipolygons for complex administrative areas, 3D geometries for terrain). It integrates directly with PostgreSQL's ACID transaction model, row-level security, and JSON support.

The GIS domain uses a dedicated PostGIS database instance, separate from the transactional core databases of other domains. This isolation is necessary because: spatial queries can be CPU and I/O intensive and must not contend with transactional workloads; spatial data has different backup and maintenance requirements; and the PostGIS instance requires specific tuning (work_mem, shared_buffers, and effective_cache_size parameters differ significantly from an OLTP-tuned PostgreSQL configuration).

### 4.2 Coordinate Reference System Strategy

All geometry in the GIS domain is stored in two coordinate reference systems managed as a dual-storage model:

**WGS84 (EPSG:4326)** — the geographic coordinate system (longitude/latitude in decimal degrees) — is the interchange standard. Data ingested from external sources (GPS tracks, satellite products, partner agency data) is normalized to WGS84 on ingest. WGS84 is used for data exchange and for rendering in web map clients (which expect longitude/latitude).

**UTM Zone 39N (EPSG:32639)** — the projected coordinate system covering Tajikistan — is used for metric calculations: area computation, distance calculation, buffer generation, and spatial analysis. WGS84 trigonometric calculations introduce distortion for metric operations; the projected system provides accurate metric results within Tajikistan's geographic extent.

Geometry columns are stored in WGS84 in the database (the web-mapping convention). Spatial functions that require metric accuracy use an explicit coordinate transformation (ST_Transform) to the projected CRS at query time. This is handled transparently by the GIS domain's query service.

### 4.3 Geometry Type Catalog

The platform manages the following primary geometry types, each suited to specific data categories:

**Point (2D and 3D):** Incident locations (reported at a specific GPS coordinate), weather station positions, seismic monitoring station positions, infrastructure nodes (bridges, pumping stations, reservoirs).

**LineString:** River channels (with flow direction as an attribute), road network, pipeline routes, power transmission lines.

**Polygon:** Administrative boundaries (districts, jamoats), flood inundation zones (at defined probability levels), landslide susceptibility zones, fire risk zones, evacuation areas, affected area boundaries for incidents.

**MultiPolygon:** Administrative regions that consist of non-contiguous areas, complex hazard zones that span multiple disconnected polygons.

**Raster (via PostGIS raster type or external raster store):** Digital Elevation Models (DEM), land use/land cover, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), soil moisture products, satellite-derived surface reflectance. Raster data at full resolution is stored in the object storage (MinIO) and registered in the GIS domain's raster catalog. Downsampled versions used for web visualization are stored in the tile server's cache.

### 4.4 Spatial Indexing Strategy

Spatial queries without indexes perform full table scans with bounding-box comparisons — prohibitively slow for datasets with thousands of geometries. The GIS domain enforces spatial indexing standards:

**GIST index on all geometry columns** (the standard PostGIS spatial index type). GIST indexes accelerate bounding-box intersection queries, which are the primary filter in most spatial queries ("find all incidents within this polygon's bounding box, then apply exact geometry test").

**Composite indexes** (spatial + attribute) for the most common query patterns: (geometry, incident_type, incident_date) for incident queries, (geometry, hazard_class, severity) for hazard zone queries. These composite indexes avoid table heap access for attribute filters that co-occur with spatial filters.

**Clustering** of frequently queried tables by the spatial index (CLUSTER command) groups geometrically proximate rows on disk, improving spatial range query performance by reducing random I/O.

**BRIN indexes** on temporal columns for time-series spatial data (incident timestamps, measurement timestamps) — BRIN indexes provide excellent performance for time-ordered data with low storage overhead.

### 4.5 Large Dataset Handling

For datasets with millions of records (dense point clouds, high-resolution administrative datasets, complete road networks), specific strategies manage query performance:

**Table partitioning by region (administrative boundary):** Incident records and environmental monitoring records are partitioned by the administrative region (oblast-level) they fall within. Region-based partitioning allows queries filtered by region to scan only the relevant partition, reducing I/O by up to 90% for regional queries.

**Geometry simplification for overview scales:** Complex polygons (detailed administrative boundaries, high-precision flood zone boundaries) are stored at full resolution for analysis but simplified versions (using Douglas-Peucker algorithm at multiple tolerance levels) are pre-computed and stored as additional geometry columns. The tile server uses the appropriate simplification level for each zoom level, preventing the client from receiving kilobytes of geometry for features that appear as small polygons on the screen.

**Materialized spatial views for common analysis patterns:** Pre-computed spatial joins (incidents joined to their administrative regions, hazard zones joined to infrastructure within them) are stored as materialized views and refreshed on a defined schedule. These avoid the cost of repeated spatial join computation for frequently used analytical views.

## 5. Map Rendering Architecture

### 5.1 Rendering Strategy: Raster Tiles vs. Vector Tiles

Both raster tile and vector tile approaches are used, serving different purposes:

**Raster tiles (PNG/JPEG)** are pre-rendered images at each zoom level. They are fast to deliver and render in the client (just display an image) but are not interactive — the client cannot style them differently or query their features. Used for: base map backgrounds, raster data products (satellite imagery, DEM hillshading, precipitation rasters), and complex multi-layer composites where per-feature interactivity is not required.

**Vector tiles (Mapbox Vector Tile format, MVT)** contain the geometry and attributes of features in a compact binary format. The client renders them using a vector rendering library (Mapbox GL JS, MapLibre GL). Vector tiles support: dynamic styling based on attribute values, feature selection and popup display, filtering by attribute, and zoom-level-appropriate label placement. Used for: incident locations, administrative boundaries, hazard zones, infrastructure, and all layers where users need to click features and query their attributes.

The architectural rule: use raster tiles for background and raster data products; use vector tiles for interactive thematic layers. This combination provides visual richness from raster backgrounds with full interactivity on vector thematic layers.

### 5.2 Tile Server Architecture

**Martin** (a Rust-based vector tile server) is the recommended tile server for on-demand vector tile generation from PostGIS. Martin connects directly to PostGIS, generates MVT tiles on request, and serves them with configurable caching headers.

For raster tiles (DEM hillshading, satellite imagery): **GeoServer** or **MapServer** handles raster tile generation. GeoServer provides WMS (Web Map Service) and WMTS (Web Map Tile Service) endpoints for standards-based map delivery, which is important for potential future integration with partner government systems that expect OGC-compliant interfaces.

**TileCache (Nginx-based):** Generated tiles are cached by Nginx using its proxy_cache directive. Cache keys are the tile coordinates (zoom/x/y) and the layer parameters. Cache TTL is configured per layer type:
- Administrative boundaries: 24-hour TTL (rarely updated)
- Hazard zones: 1-hour TTL (updated after analysis runs)
- Incident locations: 5-minute TTL (new incidents can arrive at any time)
- Satellite base map: 7-day TTL (updated infrequently)

**MBTiles pre-generation for base layers:** The base map (administrative boundaries, major roads, rivers, settlements) is pre-generated as an MBTiles archive for all zoom levels from 0 to 14. This archive is served statically by Nginx with no PostGIS involvement for base map tiles — the PostGIS database is reserved for dynamic thematic layers that require fresh data.

### 5.3 Client-Server Responsibility Division

**Server responsibilities:**
- Tile generation and serving
- Spatial query execution for feature attributes (popup data, analysis results)
- Data filtering based on authorization context (layer access control)
- WFS (Web Feature Service) endpoint for feature download
- Export generation (map image at requested extent and resolution)

**Client responsibilities:**
- Tile request and rendering (vector tile client-side styling)
- Layer visibility control
- Zoom/pan interaction
- Feature hover and click handling
- Drawing tools for user-defined geometries (incident marking, area selection)
- Legend and scale bar rendering

The critical division: spatial computation happens on the server; visual rendering of vector data happens on the client. This means the server does not need to render complex multi-layer map images for every user interaction — it serves data and the client renders it locally using GPU-accelerated vector rendering.

## 6. Data Ingestion & Pipelines

### 6.1 Ingestion Source Types

The GIS and Analytics platform ingests data from four categories of sources, each with different pipeline requirements:

**Category 1 — Manual Entry (Structured Forms):**
Field analysts and operators enter incident data through the platform's structured data collection forms (owned by the Analytics domain). Form submissions are validated at entry time against defined schemas, written to the operational database, and published as `incident.reported` events. This is the primary source of incident records.

**Category 2 — File-Based Batch Ingestion:**
Spatial datasets uploaded by analysts: administrative boundary updates, infrastructure datasets, hazard zone revisions, historical incident archives. Files are uploaded through FileManagement, registered in the GIS domain's ingestion queue, and processed through the ETL pipeline. Supported formats: GeoJSON, Shapefile (ESRI), KML, GeoPackage, CSV with coordinate columns, Excel with coordinate columns, GeoTIFF (raster).

**Category 3 — External API and Feed Integration:**
Automated ingestion from external data providers:
- **Meteorological data:** Precipitation, temperature, wind data from Tajikistan's Hydrometeorology Agency APIs or global sources (ERA5 reanalysis from Copernicus, CHIRPS rainfall data).
- **Seismic monitoring:** Real-time seismic activity feeds from the Central Asian Seismic Risk Initiative (CASRI) or global catalogs (USGS Earthquake Catalog API).
- **Remote sensing products:** Satellite-derived data products (MODIS flood mapping, Sentinel-2 burned area products, SMAP soil moisture). These are batch products delivered on defined schedules.
- **Partner agency feeds:** Data feeds from other government agencies (Ministry of Environment, Ministry of Agriculture water management data) through defined API contracts.

**Category 4 — IoT and Real-time Sensor Feeds (Future):**
River gauging station readings, landslide acceleration sensors, weather station measurements. Phase 1 supports manual data entry for sensor observations; Phase 2 introduces automated sensor data ingestion through MQTT or HTTP streaming endpoints.

### 6.2 ETL/ELT Pipeline Architecture

The platform uses a hybrid ETL/ELT approach: transformation before loading is required for spatial data (coordinate reprojection, geometry validation, format normalization); transformation after loading is preferred for analytical aggregation (where the analytical store's compute power is more efficient than pre-computation in the pipeline).

**Ingestion pipeline stages:**

**Stage 1 — Format Normalization:**
Raw data in source format is parsed and converted to a canonical intermediate representation. For spatial data: geometry is validated (ST_IsValid check), coordinate reference system is normalized to WGS84, and schema mapping is applied (source field names mapped to canonical attribute names).

**Stage 2 — Quality Validation:**
Data is checked against defined quality rules:
- Geometry validity (no self-intersecting polygons, no zero-area polygons)
- Attribute completeness (required fields present)
- Range checks (precipitation values within physically plausible bounds, coordinates within Tajikistan's bounding box)
- Temporal consistency (event timestamps not in the future beyond a defined tolerance, not before the platform's operational start date)
- Duplicate detection (same incident reported twice from different sources)

Data that fails validation is quarantined in a validation error store, not loaded into the operational database. Validation errors are surfaced to the data steward for review.

**Stage 3 — Enrichment:**
Validated records are enriched with derived attributes:
- Spatial enrichment: the administrative region (oblast, rayon, jamoat) the incident falls within is added by spatial join against the administrative boundary dataset
- Elevation attribute: the elevation above sea level at the incident location is added from the DEM
- Distance attributes: distance to nearest river, nearest road, nearest settlement are computed at ingestion time and stored to avoid repeated computation at query time

**Stage 4 — Load to Operational Store:**
Enriched records are written to the GIS domain's PostGIS database (spatial records) or the Analytics domain's operational database (incident records).

**Stage 5 — Load to Analytical Store:**
A downstream pipeline moves new records from the operational store to the analytical store (ClickHouse or TimescaleDB). This pipeline runs on a schedule (every 15 minutes for incident data, hourly for environmental data) and handles incremental loading — only new or updated records since the last pipeline run are processed.

**Stage 6 — Feature Engineering for ML:**
A separate pipeline (run after analytical store loading) computes and updates the feature store for ML models. Feature computation may involve complex aggregations: "7-day cumulative precipitation in the upstream watershed," "30-day NDVI anomaly," "number of incidents in the same region in the past 90 days." These are computed from the analytical store and loaded into the ML feature store.

### 6.3 Pipeline Orchestration

**Apache Airflow** (or its lightweight alternative, Prefect) orchestrates the pipeline DAGs (Directed Acyclic Graphs of pipeline stages). Pipeline runs are scheduled, monitored, and logged. Failed runs trigger alerts to the data steward role. Pipeline execution history is retained for data lineage tracking.

Pipeline configurations (source endpoints, validation rules, transformation logic, schedule definitions) are stored as versioned configuration files in the platform's configuration management system — pipeline changes are versioned, reviewed, and auditable.

### 6.4 Batch vs. Real-time Ingestion

**Batch ingestion** (scheduled, periodic) is used for:
- External API data pulls (meteorological daily summaries, remote sensing products)
- Analytical store updates
- Feature store computation
- Report pre-generation

**Near-real-time ingestion** (event-triggered, sub-minute latency) is used for:
- Incident reports from forms (immediate on submission)
- Seismic event feeds (immediate on publication by source)
- Urgent data uploads by analysts during active emergency response

Near-real-time ingestion uses the platform event bus: a form submission publishes an `incident.reported` event, the ingestion pipeline's event consumer picks it up within seconds, processes it through validation and enrichment, and commits it to the operational database. The incident appears on the operational map within seconds of submission.

## 7. Dataset & Layer Model

### 7.1 Dataset

A Dataset is the fundamental unit of data management in the GIS and Analytics domains. A Dataset is a cohesive collection of spatial or tabular records with a defined schema, a defined owner, a defined update cadence, and a provenance record.

Each Dataset record contains:
- **Identity:** Unique ID, name, description, keywords
- **Schema:** The attribute schema (field names, types, constraints)
- **Geometry type:** For spatial datasets, the geometry type (point, linestring, polygon, raster)
- **CRS:** Coordinate reference system of stored data
- **Owner:** The organizational position responsible for data quality and updates
- **Classification:** Data sensitivity level
- **Provenance:** Source (manual entry, external API, file upload), source URL if applicable, original source organization
- **Cadence:** How frequently the dataset is updated (real-time, daily, weekly, on-demand)
- **Version:** Current version number; version history references previous versions
- **Quality status:** Last validation run result and timestamp
- **Retention policy:** How long records are retained

### 7.2 Layer

A Layer is a configured view over one or more Datasets, designed for map visualization or analytical use. The Layer separates data management (Dataset) from data presentation (Layer) — the same Dataset can be visualized through multiple Layers with different styling, filtering, and symbolization.

A Layer record contains:
- **Source Dataset(s):** One or more datasets contributing to this layer
- **SQL filter:** An optional filter applied at query time (e.g., "incidents of type = flood AND severity >= 3")
- **Geometry column:** Which geometry column to use for visualization
- **Attribute projection:** Which attributes to include in vector tile features (exclude sensitive attributes from tiles)
- **Symbology rule:** How to style features (color classification, size scaling, label fields)
- **Scale range:** Minimum and maximum zoom levels at which this layer is visible
- **Layer type:** Vector tile layer, raster tile layer, WMS layer, or analytical aggregation layer
- **Access control:** Which roles and positions may access this layer
- **Classification:** The layer's classification level

### 7.3 Layer Categories for CoESCD

**Base layers** (administrative context, always visible to all authorized users):
- Administrative boundaries (oblast, rayon, jamoat)
- Major rivers and water bodies
- Road network (strategic roads)
- Settlements and populated places
- Elevation (terrain hillshading)

**Infrastructure layers** (restricted by classification for sensitive infrastructure):
- Critical infrastructure (power stations, water treatment, communication towers)
- Bridges and critical road infrastructure
- Hospitals and emergency facilities
- Fuel storage and hazardous materials facilities

**Hazard layers** (updated by analytical runs):
- Seismic hazard zones (by intensity level)
- Flood susceptibility zones (by return period: 10-year, 50-year, 100-year)
- Landslide susceptibility zones (by probability class)
- Fire risk zones (seasonal, updated based on drought index and vegetation conditions)
- Avalanche risk corridors

**Operational layers** (real-time, updated continuously):
- Active incident locations
- Active hazard zone boundaries (declared during an emergency event)
- Resource deployment positions (emergency response teams, equipment)
- Evacuation routes (activated during specific emergency types)

**Analytical layers** (updated by analytical runs):
- Incident density heatmaps (by type, by time period)
- Response time performance by region
- Historical incident aggregation by administrative unit

**AI/ML prediction layers** (updated by model inference runs):
- 30-day flood risk index by watershed
- Current-season landslide risk by slope unit
- Composite multi-hazard risk score by administrative unit
- Anomaly detection flags (regions with anomalous incident rates)

### 7.4 Dataset and Layer Versioning

Datasets are versioned at the publication level. When a dataset's schema changes or a major update (such as a new administrative boundary revision) is published, a new version record is created. The previous version is retained as a historical record. Layers reference a specific dataset version — a layer can be updated to use a new dataset version through explicit action, preventing silent data changes in operational layers.

Layer configurations are also versioned. When a layer's symbology, filter, or access control is changed, a new layer version is created. The previous configuration is retained, enabling rollback and change auditing.

## 8. Spatial Analytics

### 8.1 Core Spatial Query Types

The GIS domain's query service exposes the following categories of spatial queries as a service API:

**Containment queries:** "Which incidents occurred within administrative unit X?" Implemented as ST_Within or ST_Contains in PostGIS. Used for regional incident counts, regional statistics, and administrative reporting.

**Intersection queries:** "Which infrastructure assets fall within flood zone Y?" Implemented as ST_Intersects. Used for impact assessment: when a new flood zone is declared, the intersection query identifies all affected infrastructure within seconds.

**Proximity queries:** "What assets are within Z kilometers of this incident?" Implemented as ST_DWithin (which uses the spatial index effectively, unlike ST_Distance applied to all rows). Used for resource dispatch optimization, notification zone computation, and impact assessment.

**Buffer analysis:** Generate a polygon representing the area within a specified distance of a geometry. ST_Buffer in PostGIS. Used for impact zone computation (buffer around a hazardous spill location), evacuation zone generation (buffer around an incident with road network constraints), and notification zone generation.

**Overlay analysis:** Compute the geometric intersection of two polygon layers (flood zone AND high-density population zone). Implemented as ST_Intersection with ST_Area for area computation. Used for compound risk assessment: how much high-density residential area falls within the 50-year flood plain?

**Watershed/catchment analysis:** Identify the upstream drainage area contributing to a river point. This requires a hydrological network dataset and DEM-derived flow direction data. Implemented as a graph traversal over the hydrological network combined with spatial queries. Used for flood warning: upstream precipitation events that will affect downstream communities.

### 8.2 Aggregation by Region

The most common analytical operation: aggregate incident records by administrative unit to produce choropleth maps and tabular statistics. The pre-computed spatial enrichment (each incident record has its administrative region attributes added at ingest time) means region-based aggregation reduces to a standard GROUP BY SQL query without requiring a spatial join at query time.

Region-based aggregations are further accelerated by the analytical store, which maintains pre-aggregated tables at the monthly and quarterly level. For the most common dashboard queries ("incidents by rayon this year"), the analytical store query completes in milliseconds.

### 8.3 Time-based Spatial Analysis

Emergency data has a strong temporal dimension. Spatial analysis must incorporate time as a first-class query dimension:

**Point-in-time analysis:** What was the state of hazard zones on a specific date? What incidents were active on a specific date? The dataset versioning model supports this for managed layers. Incident records carry timestamps and can be filtered by time.

**Period aggregation:** How many incidents occurred in region X in year Y? The temporal partitioning of incident data (see Section 4.5) makes period-filtered queries efficient.

**Trend analysis:** How has the incident rate changed over consecutive periods? Implemented through window functions in PostgreSQL and TimescaleDB's built-in time-series aggregation functions.

**Seasonal pattern analysis:** Does landslide risk in region X peak in a consistent seasonal window? Requires extracting calendar attributes (month, week) from timestamps and aggregating across multiple years. Implemented in the analytical store with pre-computed seasonal aggregation tables.

### 8.4 Multi-layer Composite Analysis

The most analytically powerful capability: combining multiple spatial datasets to identify compound risk conditions. Examples:

**Multi-hazard overlay:** Regions where the flood susceptibility zone, the unstable slope zone, and the 100-year precipitation event zone all intersect. Produced by overlaying three polygon layers with ST_Intersection and computing the compound risk score.

**Infrastructure vulnerability assessment:** For each critical infrastructure asset, compute: which hazard zones does it fall within, what is its distance from the nearest stable access route, what is the population it serves. Produced by multi-layer intersection and attribute aggregation.

**Evacuation capacity analysis:** For a declared emergency zone, compute: how many residents are within the zone (population raster), how many evacuation routes connect to safety areas, what is the road network capacity for evacuation traffic. This combines polygon containment, road network analysis, and population estimation.

## 9. Analytical Platform

### 9.1 Analytical Store Architecture

The analytical platform requires a data store optimized for aggregation queries over large, append-mostly time-series datasets — a workload that PostgreSQL OLTP is poorly suited for.

**TimescaleDB** (a PostgreSQL extension for time-series data) is the recommended analytical store for Phase 1. TimescaleDB automatically partitions time-series tables into configurable time chunks (hypertables), provides time-series-specific aggregate functions, and supports continuous aggregates (automatically maintained pre-computed rollups). It runs as a PostgreSQL extension, reducing operational complexity — the team already manages PostgreSQL; no new database system to operate.

For Phase 2/3, if analytical query complexity and data volume exceed TimescaleDB's comfortable range, **ClickHouse** is the recommended migration target. ClickHouse is a columnar OLAP database purpose-built for analytical workloads, providing sub-second query response on billions of rows with appropriate hardware. The migration path is designed: the ETL pipeline that feeds the analytical store can target either TimescaleDB or ClickHouse without changes to the data producers.

### 9.2 Reporting Engine

Reports are pre-configured analytical products with a defined query, defined parameters, and defined output format. The reporting engine:

**Template-based generation:** Report templates define the SQL query (against the analytical store), the parameters that can be specified at generation time (time period, region, incident type), and the output layout (table, chart, map, or combination). Templates are managed by the `analytics_admin` role.

**On-demand generation:** Users with appropriate access request report generation. The reporting service executes the template query with the specified parameters and renders the output to the requested format (PDF via headless rendering, Excel via server-side workbook generation, CSV for data export).

**Scheduled generation:** Reports can be scheduled for automatic generation (daily, weekly, monthly). Generated reports are stored in FileManagement and a notification is sent to the report's designated recipients.

**Report archive:** All generated reports are archived in FileManagement with the report template ID, generation timestamp, parameters used, and the user who requested generation. The archive provides traceability: the exact query and parameters that produced a historical report are always recoverable.

### 9.3 Dashboard Architecture

Dashboards are collections of widgets, each displaying a specific analytical metric. Dashboards are role-sensitive: a dashboard template specifies which roles see which widgets, and the data in each widget is filtered by the requesting user's authority scope.

**Widget types:** Time-series chart (incident trend over time), choropleth map (metric by administrative region), bar chart (incident count by type), KPI tile (single metric: total incidents this quarter, average response time this month), ranked list (top 5 most incident-prone rayons this year), and data table (tabular incident listing with filters).

**Data freshness:** Each widget specifies its acceptable data staleness. KPI tiles on the executive dashboard may accept data that is up to 15 minutes old (from the analytical store's refresh cycle). Real-time incident counts on the operational dashboard require near-real-time data and query the operational database directly.

**Dashboard state persistence:** User-applied filters and configurations on dashboards are persisted per user, so a returning analyst does not need to reconfigure their view. Shared dashboard configurations (standardized organizational views) are managed by the `analytics_admin`.

### 9.4 Export Functionality

The analytical platform supports structured data exports for post-processing outside the platform:

**Spatial data export:** GeoJSON, Shapefile, GeoPackage, KML. Exported spatial data is filtered by the exporter's authority scope and classification clearance — a user cannot export data they cannot access.

**Tabular data export:** Excel, CSV. Incident records, analytical aggregates, and report data.

**Map export:** PNG or PDF of the current map view at specified resolution. The map export service renders the current layer configuration server-side to produce a fixed-layout image — not dependent on a connected client browser.

**Report PDF export:** Formatted analytical reports with charts, maps, and tables.

All exports are logged as audit events: who exported what data, at what scope, at what time.

## 10. AI/ML Forecasting Architecture

### 10.1 Architecture Philosophy

The AI/ML architecture is designed around two non-negotiable constraints for a government operational system:

**Constraint 1 — Explainability over complexity.** A flood risk score that analysts cannot interpret and validate will not be used operationally. Models must produce outputs accompanied by explanations: "This watershed scores High risk because upstream precipitation in the last 7 days is 220% of the historical median, soil moisture is at 85th percentile, and the snowpack is 140% of normal." Gradient Boosted Trees and similar interpretable models are preferred over deep neural networks for risk scoring. Neural networks may be appropriate for specific tasks (image classification of satellite imagery) where explainability is less operationally critical.

**Constraint 2 — Human-in-the-loop for operational decisions.** AI forecasts are analytical inputs, not automated decision triggers. No automated action (alert, evacuation order, resource deployment) is triggered solely by an AI model output. Every operational use of a model output requires a human analyst to review, validate against current conditions, and explicitly endorse the forecast before it informs operational decisions. The platform supports this workflow — model outputs arrive as draft risk layers pending analyst review, not as published operational layers.

### 10.2 ML Platform Components

**Data Platform (Feature Store):**
The feature store is a versioned, curated repository of ML-ready features derived from the GIS and Analytics data. Features are defined in a feature catalog specifying: feature name, derivation logic, data source, update cadence, and data quality metrics. The feature store serves both model training (historical features aligned by time windows) and real-time inference (current feature values for generating predictions).

**Model Training Environment:**
A Python-based ML environment (Jupyter notebooks for exploration, structured Python pipelines for production training) that consumes the feature store to train models. Training pipelines are versioned and reproducible — given the same feature store snapshot, the training pipeline produces the same model. Training pipelines are executed in the platform's compute infrastructure (not external cloud services) to maintain data sovereignty.

**Model Registry:**
A versioned catalog of trained models managed by the MLflow model registry (or equivalent open-source tool). The registry stores: the serialized model artifact, the training dataset version used, the feature set, performance metrics on the evaluation set, and deployment status (staging, production, deprecated).

**Model Serving Layer:**
Models in production status are served through a lightweight REST API endpoint hosted within the platform's infrastructure. The serving layer accepts a prediction request (a set of feature values for a specific location or region) and returns the model's output (a risk score with confidence interval and contributing feature importances). The serving layer does not perform feature computation — it receives pre-computed feature values from the prediction workflow.

**Prediction Workflow:**
A scheduled pipeline that orchestrates end-to-end prediction generation:
1. Retrieve current feature values from the feature store for all prediction units (regions, watersheds, slope units)
2. Send feature vectors to the model serving layer
3. Collect predictions
4. Write predictions to the prediction output store
5. Publish predictions to the GIS domain as draft risk layer updates
6. Notify the designated analyst that new predictions are available for review

### 10.3 Training Pipeline Design

Model training follows a structured pipeline with checkpointed stages:

**Stage 1 — Feature retrieval:** Pull the historical feature store for the training window (e.g., all records from 2010 to 2024 for the flood model).

**Stage 2 — Label alignment:** Align features to labels (which events actually became incidents, which did not). Label construction is careful about temporal leakage: features used to predict an event at time T must come from before time T.

**Stage 3 — Data splitting:** Split data into training, validation, and test sets by time (not random split — random splits create temporal data leakage). The test set uses the most recent period (holdout for final evaluation). The validation set is used during training for hyperparameter tuning.

**Stage 4 — Training:** Train the model with cross-validation on the training set.

**Stage 5 — Evaluation:** Evaluate on the test set. Record performance metrics: precision, recall, F1, AUC-ROC, calibration (are the risk score probabilities well-calibrated?), and spatial calibration (is performance uniform across regions or does accuracy vary by geography?).

**Stage 6 — Registration:** If the new model improves on the current production model on the test metrics, register it in the model registry as a candidate for promotion to staging.

**Stage 7 — Validation review:** A domain expert (an experienced emergency analyst) reviews the new model's outputs on a validation holdout, compares them to their own domain knowledge of known events, and decides whether the model's predictions align with expert expectation. Model promotion requires expert sign-off, not just metric improvement.

## 11. Forecasting Use Cases

### 11.1 Flood Risk Forecasting

**Purpose:** Estimate the probability of flood events exceeding a defined severity threshold for river basins and lowland areas in the coming 7-30 day window.

**Data requirements:**
- Historical flood event catalog (location, date, severity, duration, affected area) — minimum 10-15 years
- Daily precipitation records for upstream watersheds — same period
- River gauge measurements (daily flow rates) where available
- Soil moisture products (SMAP satellite or model-derived) — represents antecedent soil saturation
- Snowpack equivalent measurements for mountain watersheds (snow water equivalent, SWE) — critical for spring snowmelt flood prediction
- DEM-derived watershed boundaries and channel network
- Land use/land cover (impervious surface fraction affects runoff)

**Feature engineering:**
- 7-day, 14-day, and 30-day cumulative precipitation anomaly (relative to historical median for the same calendar period)
- Soil moisture percentile (0-100 scale relative to historical distribution)
- Snowpack anomaly (current SWE vs. historical median)
- Days since last significant precipitation (soil antecedent moisture proxy when satellite products are unavailable)
- Terrain-derived slope and drainage density within the watershed
- Historical flood frequency in the target unit (empirical recurrence rate)

**Model type:** Gradient Boosted Tree (XGBoost/LightGBM) for risk scoring by watershed. Logistic regression as a baseline and interpretability benchmark. Probabilistic calibration applied to convert model scores to probability estimates.

**Output:** A risk score (0-1) per watershed per forecast period, with confidence intervals and feature importance decomposition (what factors drive the high score for this specific watershed this week).

**Validation approach:** Backtesting on held-out years; comparison with historical flood event dates; evaluation of precision/recall at operational threshold.

### 11.2 Landslide Risk Assessment

**Purpose:** Identify slopes with elevated landslide probability given current and forecasted precipitation conditions.

**Data requirements:**
- Historical landslide inventory (location, date, type: rotational, translational, debris flow, extent)
- High-resolution DEM (10m or better) for slope gradient, aspect, and curvature computation
- Geology map (lithology, fault proximity) — critical factor for slope stability
- Daily precipitation records
- Vegetation cover (NDVI time series — vegetation loss reduces slope cohesion)
- Seismic event catalog (seismic triggering is a major landslide cause in Tajikistan)

**Feature engineering:**
- Slope gradient and aspect (terrain-derived, static)
- Plan curvature and profile curvature (terrain-derived, static)
- Geology class (categorical, static)
- Distance to nearest fault (static)
- Antecedent precipitation (7-day cumulative)
- NDVI anomaly (current vs. historical baseline for the calendar period)
- Days since last significant seismic event within 50km
- Historical landslide density within 5km (proximity to known unstable areas)

**Prediction unit:** The slope unit (defined by terrain analysis as a homogeneous hillslope polygon) rather than an administrative unit — landslide risk is fundamentally terrain-dependent, not administratively bounded.

**Model type:** Random Forest for interpretability and robustness; SVM as a comparison model for binary susceptibility classification. Susceptibility map generation from model outputs, interpolated across the full slope unit inventory.

### 11.3 Fire Risk Assessment

**Purpose:** Estimate fire ignition probability and potential spread area by region during fire-prone periods (May-October).

**Data requirements:**
- Historical fire event catalog (ignition location, date, burned area, fire type)
- NDVI time series (dry vegetation indicator)
- Precipitation deficit (days without significant rainfall)
- Temperature records (extreme heat events)
- Wind speed and direction (fire spread driver)
- Land use/land cover (fuel type classification)
- Human activity proximity (ignition source probability)

**Feature engineering:**
- Keetch-Byram Drought Index (KBDI) — a continuous fire weather index based on temperature and precipitation
- NDVI anomaly (current vs. long-term baseline)
- Days since last precipitation > 10mm
- Maximum temperature in the past 7 days
- Wind speed (current and forecast)
- Land cover type (fuel load category)
- Population density within 2km (anthropogenic ignition probability)

**Model type:** Ensemble of logistic regression (interpretable risk factor weighting) and Random Forest. Spatial autocorrelation-aware validation (Moran's I for residual spatial clustering check).

### 11.4 Seismic Impact Estimation

**Purpose:** Rapidly estimate the potential impact of detected seismic events on population and infrastructure before on-the-ground assessment is available.

This use case is different from the others: it is triggered by an event (a seismic detection), not run on a schedule. The model runs within minutes of a seismic event being detected in the feed.

**Data requirements:**
- Seismic event parameters (magnitude, depth, epicenter, focal mechanism) from the seismic monitoring feed
- Population distribution (gridded population product)
- Building inventory with vulnerability class (reinforced concrete, unreinforced masonry — by far the most common and most vulnerable in Tajikistan)
- Critical infrastructure locations and vulnerability class
- Historical earthquake damage reports (ground truth for model calibration)
- Soil type map (amplification factor by soil class)

**Feature engineering:**
- Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) estimated from GMPEs (Ground Motion Prediction Equations) as a function of magnitude, distance, and depth
- Local soil amplification factor (from site class map)
- Population exposure by intensity zone
- Infrastructure count by intensity zone and vulnerability class

**Output:** Rapid impact estimate with uncertainty bounds: estimated number of potentially affected people, probability of structural damage by severity class, alert level recommendation (1-5), and a map of estimated intensity zones.

**This is not a deterministic seismic analysis tool** — it is a rapid initial assessment model that informs early response prioritization. Detailed engineering assessment requires specialist geotechnical analysis. The model's role is to answer "how serious might this be?" in the first 15 minutes after a seismic event, before field reports arrive.

### 11.5 Anomaly Detection

**Purpose:** Identify regions with anomalous incident reporting rates relative to historical baselines — potential early indicators of emerging situations that do not yet meet formal emergency criteria.

**Model type:** Statistical process control (CUSUM/EWMA charts for time-series anomaly detection) and Isolation Forest for multivariate anomaly detection. These methods are more appropriate than deep learning for the relatively sparse incident data available.

**Signals monitored:**
- Incident count anomaly by region and type (statistically unusual incident rate)
- Unusual spatial clustering of incidents (new spatial hotspot emergence)
- Unusual combination of hazard precursor indicators (multiple risk factors elevated simultaneously in the same region)

**Output:** Anomaly flags in the operational dashboard: "Rasht Valley is showing incident report rate 3.2 standard deviations above the seasonal baseline for the past 14 days."

## 12. GIS + AI Integration

### 12.1 Risk Layer Publication Workflow

AI model outputs enter the GIS platform through a governed publication workflow, not through direct database writes:

1. The prediction pipeline completes and writes outputs to the AI/ML domain's prediction output store
2. A `prediction.batch_complete` event is published to the platform event bus
3. The GIS domain receives the event and creates a **draft risk layer** from the prediction outputs
4. The draft layer is stored in the GIS database with status `DRAFT` — it is not visible to operational users
5. The GIS domain notifies the designated analyst review queue: "New flood risk prediction layer is available for review"
6. An authorized analyst reviews the draft layer: they inspect the predictions, compare them against current operational knowledge, and check for obvious anomalies (a high-risk score in a region that is currently in drought, for example)
7. The analyst approves or rejects the layer. Approval transitions the layer to `OPERATIONAL` status and makes it visible to all authorized users
8. If rejected, the analyst records the reason; the prediction pipeline team investigates before the next prediction run

This workflow ensures no AI output reaches operational map viewers without analyst validation. The platform records the analyst who approved each prediction layer — the human accountability for operational use of AI outputs is explicit.

### 12.2 Risk Score Visualization

Risk scores from AI models are visualized as classified polygon layers with:
- **Color classification:** A diverging color scheme from low (green/blue) to high (orange/red) risk, with five classification breaks by default. Classification breaks are defined by the model's output distribution — not hard-coded — and are recalculated when a new prediction batch is published.
- **Transparency control:** Risk layers are rendered as semi-transparent polygons overlaid on the base map, so underlying administrative and infrastructure layers remain visible.
- **Confidence interval visualization:** Where models produce confidence bounds, the layer can display the confidence interval as a second attribute (darker color = higher confidence in the risk score).
- **Feature importance callout:** When a user queries a specific region's risk score, the popup shows not just the score but the top contributing features: "High risk driven by: soil moisture (85th percentile), upstream 14-day precipitation anomaly (+180%), snowpack (150% of normal)."

### 12.3 Temporal Risk Layer Navigation

Risk predictions are specific to a prediction date. The GIS interface supports temporal navigation of risk layers: a time slider allows analysts to compare current predictions with predictions from previous weeks. This comparison is operationally important for trend identification: is the risk for a specific watershed increasing, decreasing, or stable across successive forecast windows?

Temporal risk layer comparison is also used for model validation: comparing model predictions from past forecast runs with the actual incident records that occurred during those forecast periods.

## 13. Data Storage Strategy

### 13.1 Storage Layer Architecture

The GIS and Analytics platform uses five distinct storage layers:

**Operational Spatial Database (PostGIS):**
All vector geometry and spatial datasets used for operational map visualization and spatial queries. Optimized for spatial query performance (indexed, clustered, GIST indexes). Contains: incident locations (current + recent), hazard zones (all published versions), infrastructure datasets, administrative boundaries, hydrological network, road network.

**Time-series Analytical Store (TimescaleDB):**
Append-mostly incident records and environmental monitoring data structured for time-series analytics. Contains: incident event records (full history), weather observation records, seismic event records, river gauge measurements, AI prediction records. Optimized for time-filtered aggregation queries using continuous aggregates and materialized rollups.

**ML Feature Store (PostgreSQL + specialized format):**
Curated, versioned features for ML model training and inference. Features are stored in a tabular format keyed by (location_id, prediction_unit_id, timestamp). Access is primarily by prediction unit (all features for a given region at a given time). A Feast feature store or a custom implementation using PostgreSQL with columnar access patterns.

**Raster and Imagery Storage (MinIO object storage):**
Large raster files (full-resolution DEM, satellite imagery, rainfall products, land cover rasters). The PostGIS raster catalog holds metadata and downsampled overviews; MinIO holds the full-resolution data. Accessed through the GIS domain's raster service which streams appropriate resolution products based on the requested zoom level and analysis precision.

**Tile Cache (Nginx disk cache + optional Redis for hot tiles):**
Rendered map tiles at all zoom levels. Nginx stores the disk-based tile cache. Frequently accessed tiles (major cities, high-traffic regions) are additionally cached in Redis for sub-millisecond serving. Cache is invalidated by layer update events.

### 13.2 Data Retention Policy

**Operational spatial data:** Current versions retained indefinitely. Previous versions retained for a minimum of 7 years (government record-keeping standard) then subject to review.

**Incident records:** Retained permanently. Emergency incident records are institutional history and may have legal, insurance, and policy significance years after the event.

**Environmental monitoring data:** Full-resolution observations retained for 5 years; aggregated summaries (daily, monthly) retained permanently.

**ML training data snapshots:** Each training run's feature store snapshot is retained for 2 years (enough to retrain the model and reproduce results if needed). Older snapshots are archived.

**AI prediction outputs:** All prediction batches retained permanently as a historical record of what the system predicted and when. This enables retrospective analysis of forecast accuracy.

**Map tiles:** Cache (ephemeral, evicted by TTL or LRU). No permanent retention — tiles are regenerated from source data.

## 14. Access Control Model

### 14.1 Dataset-Level Permissions

Each dataset in the GIS domain has an access control record specifying which roles and clearance levels may:
- **View:** See the dataset exists and read its metadata
- **Query:** Execute spatial queries against the dataset
- **Export:** Download the dataset's data in any supported format
- **Edit:** Modify the dataset's records (insert, update) through the data management interface
- **Admin:** Change the dataset's access control, schema, or ownership

Dataset-level permissions are evaluated by the Authorization domain's `can()` API. The GIS domain passes the requesting user's identity and the dataset ID; the Authorization domain evaluates against the dataset's configured permissions.

### 14.2 Layer-Level Permissions

Layers have independent access control from their underlying datasets. A dataset may contain sensitive attributes, while a derived layer exposes only non-sensitive attributes from the same data. For example: the infrastructure dataset contains detailed operational specifications for power facilities (sensitive); the infrastructure base layer exposes only location, name, and type (appropriate for broad distribution).

Access to vector tile content respects classification: a user requesting tiles for a layer whose classification exceeds their clearance receives an access-denied response. The tile URL structure does not reveal whether tiles exist — an unauthorized user receives the same response as for a non-existent tile.

### 14.3 Region-Based Access

Some analytical data is access-controlled by region. A regional coordinator may have access to incident data for their oblast but not for other oblasts. Region-based access is implemented as a row-level security policy in PostgreSQL: the database enforces a WHERE clause on the administrative region code matching the user's authorized region set. This enforcement is at the database level — even a misconfigured query cannot bypass the region filter.

### 14.4 AI Prediction Layer Access

AI prediction layers are classified based on the sensitivity of the data used in the underlying model. A flood risk layer derived from publicly available meteorological data may be accessible to all platform users. A compound vulnerability layer that incorporates infrastructure sensitivity data may require restricted clearance. The analyst who approves a prediction layer designates its classification level at approval time.

## 15. Performance Optimization

### 15.1 Spatial Query Optimization

**GIST index mandatory:** Every geometry column used in spatial queries must have a GIST spatial index. The GIS domain's database migration pipeline enforces this rule — a schema change that adds a geometry column without adding a GIST index fails the migration health check.

**Two-stage spatial query pattern:** PostGIS spatial queries execute in two stages: a bounding box filter (fast, uses the GIST index) followed by an exact geometry test (slower, examines actual geometry). For complex polygon-on-polygon operations, the bounding box filter eliminates most candidates, making the exact geometry stage fast. The query planner uses this automatically when indexes are present.

**Pre-computed spatial attributes at ingest:** Distance to nearest river, administrative region membership, elevation, and other commonly queried spatial attributes are computed at ingest time and stored as regular table columns. These avoid repeated spatial computation at query time for the most common analysis patterns.

**Connection pooling for spatial queries:** Spatial queries hold database connections for potentially long periods. PgBouncer in transaction pooling mode reduces the number of actual database connections while still providing query throughput. The GIS domain's query service uses a dedicated connection pool separate from the transactional domains' connection pools.

### 15.2 Tile Caching Strategy

**Pre-seeding for base layers:** The static base map tiles (administrative boundaries, roads, rivers) for zoom levels 0-12 are pre-seeded into the tile cache on first deployment and after any base layer update. Pre-seeding eliminates cold-cache misses for the most frequently requested tiles.

**Layered cache TTL:** Different cache TTL for different layer volatility:
- Static base layers: 24-hour TTL
- Hazard zone layers: 1-hour TTL (update after analytical runs)
- Incident layers: 5-minute TTL
- AI risk layers: 15-minute TTL after publication

**Edge caching with Nginx:** The tile cache is served directly by Nginx without hitting the application server. A cache hit returns the pre-rendered tile in under 5ms. Only cache misses hit Martin or GeoServer for tile generation.

### 15.3 Analytical Query Optimization

**Continuous aggregates (TimescaleDB):** The most common dashboard queries (incident counts by month, by region, by type) are served from continuously maintained materialized views in TimescaleDB. These views are updated automatically on a configurable schedule (every 15 minutes). Dashboard widgets that tolerate 15-minute staleness query the materialized views; widgets requiring fresher data query the raw tables with an appropriate index scan.

**Query result caching:** Dashboard widget queries with high repeatability (the same query executed by many users with the same parameters) are cached in Redis with a TTL matching the acceptable data freshness for that widget type. The first user who runs the query pays the full query cost; subsequent users receive the cached result.

**Analytical query isolation:** Heavy analytical queries (annual trend analysis, multi-region comparative reports) are executed against the analytical store's read replica, not the primary. This prevents long-running analytical queries from blocking dashboard queries on the primary.

## 17. Real-time vs Batch Strategy

### 17.1 When Real-time is Required

**Incident location display on the operational map:** The operational map must show newly reported incidents within seconds of reporting. Near-real-time ingestion (event-driven, latency < 30 seconds from form submission to map visibility) is required. Batch processing is unacceptable for this use case — a 15-minute lag on an active emergency incident map is operationally dangerous.

**Active hazard zone propagation:** When a new flood zone is declared or an existing zone boundary is updated, the change must be visible on the operational map immediately. This is handled through event-driven cache invalidation: the zone update triggers a tile cache invalidation for the affected zoom levels, forcing fresh tile generation.

**Seismic event detection and impact estimation:** The seismic impact model runs within 5 minutes of a seismic event being detected in the feed. Seismic events are genuinely time-critical — the first 30 minutes after a major earthquake are operationally decisive for resource mobilization.

**Emergency alert delivery:** Spatial proximity alerts (new incident in a monitored region) are delivered in real-time through the Communication domain's event pipeline.

### 17.2 When Batch is Sufficient

**AI flood/landslide/fire risk forecasts:** Risk forecasts are not real-time products. A flood risk forecast computed once per day (for the coming 7-30 day window) is operationally appropriate. Daily batch processing provides sufficient freshness while allowing the computation-intensive model inference to run during off-peak hours.

**Analytical dashboard data:** Executive and departmental dashboards display data aggregated by day, week, or month. These aggregates are computed on a 15-minute batch schedule. A 15-minute data lag on a "incidents this quarter by region" widget is operationally acceptable.

**Report generation:** Scheduled reports (weekly situation reports, monthly performance reviews) are generated in nightly batch runs, not on demand, to avoid load spikes during operational hours.

**Feature store updates:** ML feature computation (complex multi-day, multi-dataset aggregations) runs in a scheduled batch pipeline after the analytical store is updated, typically once per day.

**Spatial enrichment of historical data:** When a new administrative boundary dataset is published (a rayon boundary revision), backfilling the administrative region attribute on historical incident records runs as a batch process — it can take minutes for large datasets but does not need to be instantaneous.

## 18. Data Governance

### 18.1 Data Ownership Model

Every dataset in the GIS and Analytics platform has a designated data steward — an organizational position responsible for the dataset's quality, currency, and appropriate access control. Data stewardship is recorded in the dataset catalog and is a formal organizational responsibility, not just a platform configuration.

The data steward:
- Reviews and resolves validation errors surfaced by the ingestion pipeline
- Approves new versions of the dataset for publication to operational layers
- Maintains the dataset's access control configuration
- Attests to data quality during periodic governance reviews

When a position changes occupancy, the data steward role for all datasets owned by that position is flagged for reassignment — datasets cannot be without a steward.

### 18.2 Data Lineage

The platform maintains a data lineage graph: for every dataset, the record of where its data came from, what transformations were applied, and what downstream datasets and models depend on it.

Lineage is recorded at two levels:
- **Dataset lineage:** Dataset B was derived from Dataset A through Pipeline P at time T. Dataset A was ingested from Source S on date D.
- **Feature lineage:** ML Feature F was computed from Dataset A and Dataset B using Transformation T as of time T.

When a source dataset is updated or its quality issues are discovered, the lineage graph allows immediate identification of all downstream datasets, layers, and ML models that may be affected.

### 18.3 Data Quality Metrics and SLAs

Each dataset has defined data quality metrics:
- **Completeness:** Percentage of records with no null values in required fields
- **Timeliness:** How current is the most recent record relative to the expected update cadence
- **Accuracy:** For datasets with external reference points, the percentage of records within acceptable tolerance
- **Consistency:** Internal consistency checks (incident end date not before start date; latitude within Tajikistan's bounding box)

Quality metrics are computed on each pipeline run and stored in a quality metrics history table. Dashboards for the `data_steward` role show quality trend lines per dataset. Degradation below defined quality SLA thresholds triggers alerts to the data steward.

### 18.4 Audit of Data Changes

Data modification events in the GIS and Analytics domains are emitted to the platform Audit domain:
- Dataset record created, updated, deleted
- Dataset version published to operational status
- Layer access control changed
- Validation error overridden (a data steward accepts a record that failed automated validation — always requires justification)
- Data steward assignment changed

The audit record for data changes includes: the actor, the timestamp, the specific dataset/record affected, the change type, and the previous value. This provides a complete data change history for regulatory compliance and incident investigation.

## 19. Scalability Strategy

### 19.1 Scaling the Spatial Data Platform

**Read scaling:** Read replicas of the PostGIS database serve map tile generation and spatial query traffic. Tile generation reads are read-only workloads that can be distributed across replicas without consistency concerns. Incident queries for dashboard display and spatial analytics run on replicas, reserving the primary for write operations.

**Partitioning for large datasets:** Incident records and environmental monitoring records are partitioned by administrative region and by year. Queries filtered by region and time period scan only the relevant partitions. Partition pruning is automatic when filter columns match partition keys.

**Tile server scaling:** The tile server (Martin) is stateless — additional instances can be added behind the load balancer as tile generation demand grows. The tile cache (Nginx) is the primary performance mechanism; scaling the tile server is only needed when the cache miss rate is high (during layer updates when the cache is being rebuilt).

### 19.2 Scaling the Analytical Platform

**TimescaleDB scaling path:** TimescaleDB scales vertically (more CPU and memory for the PostgreSQL process) before requiring horizontal scaling. For CoESCD's data volumes (tens of millions of incident and environmental records over a 10-year history), a well-tuned TimescaleDB instance on adequate hardware handles the analytical workload comfortably.

**ClickHouse migration path:** If analytical query complexity and data volume grow to require sub-second response on billions of rows with complex analytical SQL (multi-join aggregations across large time windows), migration to ClickHouse is the recommended path. The ETL pipeline that feeds TimescaleDB is retargeted to ClickHouse; the reporting and dashboard layers query ClickHouse through the same internal query API.

### 19.3 Scaling the ML Platform

**Training scaling:** Model training runs are CPU-intensive batch processes. They run during off-peak hours and can be parallelized across multiple CPU cores. For Phase 1, training runs fit comfortably on a dedicated multi-core server. If training time becomes a constraint (as more complex models or larger training datasets are added), training workloads can be distributed using Dask (a Python parallel computing library) or migrated to a compute cluster.

**Inference scaling:** Model inference (generating predictions for all watersheds, all slope units) is a batch operation run once per day per model. Inference throughput is not a constraint at this scale — a complete prediction run for all spatial units in Tajikistan completes in minutes on a single server. Real-time inference (the seismic impact model triggered by an event) runs on a dedicated serving endpoint that is sized for single-request latency, not throughput.

**Data volume scaling:** The feature store grows linearly with time. Historical features for a 10-year training window at daily granularity across ~500 spatial prediction units is a manageable dataset. Feature store partitioning by prediction unit and time window handles the growth gracefully.

## 20. Risks and Trade-offs

### Risk 1: Model accuracy insufficient for operational use

**Risk:** AI risk models produce predictions that are so frequently wrong (false positives triggering unnecessary preparedness actions, false negatives missing actual events) that analysts lose confidence and stop using them.

**Mitigation:** The human-in-the-loop approval workflow means poor model performance is caught before operational deployment. Model performance metrics are tracked continuously and displayed on the data steward dashboard. Retraining is triggered when performance metrics degrade below defined thresholds. Critically, model outputs are positioned as analytical inputs, not automated decisions — an analyst who disagrees with a risk score can reject the layer and note the discrepancy, contributing to model improvement.

---

### Risk 2: Data quality of historical incident catalog limits model accuracy

**Risk:** If CoESCD's historical incident records are incomplete, inconsistently categorized, or poorly georeferenced, model training data is insufficient for reliable forecasting.

**Mitigation:** The ML platform's launch is contingent on a historical data quality assessment. Before any model enters production, the training dataset is reviewed for: coverage (years of history, geographic completeness), consistency (incident type taxonomy applied consistently over time), and spatial accuracy (coordinates within plausible range, not all incidents geocoded to a district centroid). If the historical data quality is insufficient for a specific model type, that model is not deployed until the training data meets minimum quality standards. The data collection forms are designed from day one to capture the structured attributes that ML models will need.

---

### Risk 3: External data source reliability

**Risk:** The ingestion pipeline depends on external meteorological, seismic, and satellite data providers. Source API outages, format changes, or data gaps create holes in the feature store that degrade model performance.

**Mitigation:** All external data sources have defined fallback behavior:
- If the primary meteorological API is unavailable, the pipeline falls back to a secondary source (different provider or local meteorological agency data)
- If neither is available, the feature store records missing data for that time period rather than using a stale value
- Models are trained to handle missing features gracefully (imputation using historical medians for the calendar period)
- Data freshness monitoring alerts the data steward team when an external source has not updated within its expected cadence

---

### Risk 4: Spatial data sovereignty and classification conflicts

**Risk:** Some GIS datasets (critical infrastructure locations, military-sensitive facilities) are highly sensitive and their classification requirements conflict with the broad operational visibility needed for emergency response. If infrastructure locations are classified at a level that emergency responders cannot access, the platform cannot support effective response operations.

**Mitigation:** A tiered infrastructure dataset strategy: a `PUBLIC_INFRASTRUCTURE` layer with sufficient detail for emergency response but without sensitive operational specifications; a `CLASSIFIED_INFRASTRUCTURE` layer with complete operational data restricted to users with the required clearance. Emergency response needs are served by the public layer. Sensitive details are available to cleared officials through the classified layer. The classification boundary between these two datasets requires explicit policy definition by CoESCD's information security authority.

---

### Trade-off: Tile-based vs. on-demand vector rendering

Tile-based rendering (pre-computed or cached tiles) provides fast, consistent performance but introduces a staleness lag: when an incident is reported, the tiles showing the incident do not refresh until the cache expires or is explicitly invalidated. On-demand rendering from live PostGIS provides real-time accuracy but is CPU-expensive for complex multi-layer maps.

The architecture uses a hybrid: static base layers use long-TTL tiles; operational layers (incidents, active hazard zones) use short-TTL tiles (5 minutes) with event-driven invalidation for the most critical updates. This balances performance with operational data currency.

---

### Trade-off: Interpretable models vs. potentially more accurate deep learning

Gradient Boosted Trees and Random Forests are recommended over deep neural networks for risk scoring. The accuracy trade-off (deep learning may achieve higher AUC on some datasets) is accepted because: explainability is operationally required, training data volumes (10-15 years of incidents in a small country) do not justify deep learning's data requirements, and model maintenance (retraining, debugging, monitoring) is more tractable with interpretable models for a government analytics team without ML infrastructure expertise.

## 21. Final GIS & Analytics Architecture

The CoESCD GIS, Analytics, and AI Forecasting system is a **three-layer spatial intelligence platform** governed by six architectural commitments that define its character as a national emergency management intelligence system:

---

**Commitment 1 — Spatial data is a first-class institutional asset.**
The GIS domain is not a visualization layer — it is a data platform that owns, versions, governs, and manages Tajikistan's emergency management spatial knowledge base. Administrative boundaries, hazard zones, incident records, infrastructure datasets, and risk layers are formally owned, steward-assigned, versioned, and governed. The platform maintains provenance for every dataset: where it came from, what transformations produced it, and what depends on it. Spatial data governance is as rigorous as document management.

---

**Commitment 2 — The operational map reflects reality, not cache artifacts.**
Incident reports appear on the operational map within 30 seconds of submission. Active hazard zone declarations invalidate tile caches immediately. Seismic event impact estimates are available within 5 minutes of event detection. The distinction between what requires real-time currency (operational layers) and what tolerates batch latency (analytical layers, risk forecasts) is explicit and enforced by the architecture.

---

**Commitment 3 — AI forecasts are analyst-reviewed products, not automated triggers.**
No prediction layer from any ML model reaches operational map viewers without explicit analyst review and approval. The platform enforces this through the draft layer workflow — AI outputs arrive as drafts, not as published layers. The analyst who approves each risk layer is named in the layer's metadata. AI models are decision support tools; human analysts hold decision authority.

---

**Commitment 4 — The data platform feeds itself.**
External meteorological feeds, seismic catalogs, and satellite data products are ingested automatically on defined schedules. The feature store is updated from the analytical store. Model inference is scheduled. The platform does not require manual data preparation for routine analytical operations — it actively maintains its own knowledge base. Analyst effort is directed at interpretation and decision support, not at data assembly.

---

**Commitment 5 — The analytical and ML layers are designed to grow.**
TimescaleDB is the Phase 1 analytical store with a defined migration path to ClickHouse. Gradient Boosted Trees are the Phase 1 ML models with a defined extension path to more complex models as training data accumulates. The feature store's schema is designed to accommodate new environmental data sources. The model registry accommodates multiple models per hazard type. Growth is planned, not improvised.

---

**Commitment 6 — Every analytical action is auditable.**
Dataset exports, layer access, AI prediction approvals, data quality overrides, and model deployments are all auditable events. The organization can answer: who exported the infrastructure layer, when, and at what scope. Who approved the flood risk layer published on the day before last week's emergency response. What model version was in production when the March 2027 flood forecast was generated. This auditability is both a governance requirement and an operational learning tool.
